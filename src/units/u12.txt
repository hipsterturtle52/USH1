## Why this unit matters
**Reconstruction** (1865–1877) asked: How would the Confederate states rejoin the Union, and what would freedom mean for four million formerly enslaved people? The answers produced three constitutional amendments that still shape American law — the **13th, 14th, and 15th** — a battle between **President Andrew Johnson** and the **Radical Republicans**, the first Black officeholders in the South, and then a violent backlash that ended Reconstruction with the **Compromise of 1877**. The exam heavily tests **the amendments**, **competing plans**, **Black Codes**, **sharecropping**, and **why Reconstruction ended**.

## Lincoln's plan
In December 1863, Lincoln announced his lenient **Ten Percent Plan**: a Confederate state could form a new government once **10% of its 1860 voters** swore loyalty to the Union and accepted emancipation. Most Confederates would be pardoned. Lincoln's goal was a quick reunion.

Radical Republicans in Congress thought this far too easy. Their **Wade–Davis Bill (1864)** required a **majority** of a state's white men to take a loyalty oath and barred anyone who had voluntarily supported the Confederacy from voting for the new state convention. Lincoln killed it with a **pocket veto**. The dispute raised a lasting question: Was Reconstruction the job of the **president** or of **Congress**?

In March 1865, Congress created the **Freedmen's Bureau**, the first major federal social-welfare agency. Headed by General **Oliver O. Howard**, it provided food, clothing, and medical care, negotiated **labor contracts**, and — its greatest success — founded **schools** for freed people, working with northern missionary societies to establish colleges such as **Howard University** and **Fisk University**. But it never achieved meaningful **land redistribution**. General Sherman's **Field Order No. 15** (January 1865) had set aside coastal land for freed families in 40-acre plots — the origin of the hope for "**forty acres and a mule**" — but President Johnson returned most of that land to its former owners.

## Johnson's plan (Presidential Reconstruction, 1865)
After Lincoln's assassination, **Andrew Johnson**, a Tennessee Democrat who had stayed loyal to the Union, became president. Johnson hated the planter elite but also held deeply racist views and strongly supported **states' rights**. His plan required southern states to:
- repeal or void their **secession** ordinances,
- ratify the **13th Amendment**, and
- repudiate Confederate war debts.

Wealthy Confederates had to apply to Johnson personally for **pardons** — and he granted them by the thousands. Southern voters promptly elected former Confederate leaders to Congress, including the Confederacy's vice president, **Alexander Stephens**.

The new southern governments passed **Black Codes** — laws that restricted freedpeople's lives in ways that resembled slavery. They required Black Southerners to sign yearly **labor contracts**, allowed arrest for "**vagrancy**" (unemployment) with punishment by forced labor, limited the jobs they could hold and their right to own or rent land, and barred them from serving on juries or testifying against whites.

=> Johnson's lenient plan returns former Confederates to power | Black Codes (1865–1866) restrict freedpeople | Northern outrage; Congress takes control of Reconstruction from the president

When Congress met in December 1865, it **refused to seat** the newly elected southern representatives.

## Congress strikes back
Johnson **vetoed** a bill extending the Freedmen's Bureau and the **Civil Rights Act of 1866**, which declared Black Americans citizens with equal legal rights. Congress **overrode** the Civil Rights Act veto — the first time it overrode a president on a major bill.

To put these rights beyond the reach of future majorities, Congress passed the **14th Amendment** (1866; ratified **1868**):
- All persons **born or naturalized** in the United States are **citizens** (overturning **Dred Scott**).
- No state may deny any person "**due process of law**" or "the **equal protection of the laws**."
- States that denied adult male citizens the vote would lose representation in Congress.
- Former Confederate officials were barred from office unless Congress removed the ban, and the Confederate debt was declared void.

Johnson urged southern states to **reject** the amendment, and all but Tennessee did. He then campaigned against Radicals in the **1866 midterm elections** with an angry speaking tour ("swing around the circle"). Northern voters responded by giving Republicans a **veto-proof majority** in Congress.

## Radical (Congressional) Reconstruction
**Radical Republicans** — led by Representative **Thaddeus Stevens** and Senator **Charles Sumner** — wanted to transform the South, protect freedpeople's rights, and punish secession. Their **Military Reconstruction Act of 1867**:
- divided the ten unreconstructed southern states into **five military districts** under Union generals;
- required each state to write a new constitution guaranteeing **Black male suffrage**;
- required each to ratify the **14th Amendment** before being readmitted.

Congress also tried to limit Johnson. The **Tenure of Office Act (1867)** barred the president from firing cabinet officers without Senate approval. When Johnson fired Secretary of War **Edwin Stanton**, a Radical ally, the House **impeached** Johnson in **1868** — the first presidential impeachment. The Senate **acquitted** him by **one vote** short of the two-thirds needed to remove him. Johnson finished his term with little power.

In **1868**, war hero **Ulysses S. Grant** won the presidency, his popular margin made possible by about 500,000 new Black voters in the South. Congress then passed the **15th Amendment** (1869; ratified **1870**): the right to vote cannot be denied "on account of **race, color, or previous condition of servitude**." It did not ban **literacy tests, poll taxes, or property requirements**, loopholes the South later exploited — and it did not include **women**.

The women's movement split over the 15th Amendment. **Elizabeth Cady Stanton** and **Susan B. Anthony** opposed it for excluding women and formed the **National Woman Suffrage Association**; **Lucy Stone** and others supported it and formed the **American Woman Suffrage Association** (both 1869).

!! Know the three amendments cold: **13th** = abolished slavery; **14th** = citizenship + equal protection + due process; **15th** = voting cannot be denied by race. Mnemonic: *free, citizen, vote*.

## Reconstruction governments in the South
Under the new constitutions, southern **Republican** governments came to power, supported by three groups:
- **Freedmen**, who voted in large numbers. About **2,000** African Americans held public office; **Hiram Revels** of Mississippi became the first Black **US senator** (1870), followed by **Blanche K. Bruce**. African Americans held a majority only in the lower house of South Carolina's legislature and never controlled any state government.
- "**Carpetbaggers**" — the insulting name for northerners who moved south (teachers, businessmen, Union veterans, Freedmen's Bureau agents).
- "**Scalawags**" — the insulting name for white southern Republicans, often small farmers from the hill country who had opposed secession.

These governments created the South's first **public school systems**, rebuilt railroads, expanded the rights of women to property, and opened hospitals and orphanages. They raised taxes and some officials were corrupt — charges that opponents exaggerated to discredit Black political power.

## Freedom in everyday life
Freedpeople acted quickly on their freedom. They traveled to **reunite families** separated by slavery and legalized marriages. They withdrew from white-controlled churches to build their own, especially the **African Methodist Episcopal (AME)** Church and Baptist congregations, which became centers of community life. They flocked to **schools** — adults and children alike.

Economically, freedpeople wanted **land**; planters wanted controlled labor. Without land redistribution, most freedpeople became **sharecroppers**. A landowner provided land, a cabin, and often tools and seed; the sharecropper farmed it and gave a share of the crop (often half) as rent. Many also became **tenant farmers** who paid rent. Local merchants sold supplies on credit secured by the future crop (the **crop-lien system**), at high interest. Low cotton prices and debts trapped many Black and poor white farmers in a **cycle of debt** that tied them to the land almost like the old system.

=> Freedpeople lack land; planters lack cash to pay wages | Sharecropping and the crop-lien system | Cycle of debt; continued dependence on cotton and on landowners

## White resistance and the retreat from Reconstruction
Southern Democrats who wanted to restore white supremacy called themselves "**Redeemers**." Terrorist groups — above all the **Ku Klux Klan** (founded 1866 in Tennessee) — beat and murdered Black voters, teachers, and white Republicans. Congress responded with the **Enforcement Acts (1870–1871)**, including the **Ku Klux Klan Act**, and Grant used federal troops and prosecutions to break up the Klan. But violence by other groups continued, notably the **Colfax Massacre** in Louisiana (1873), where scores of Black men were killed.

Northern support for Reconstruction faded for several reasons:
- **Scandals** in Grant's administration, such as **Crédit Mobilier** (a railroad construction company that bribed congressmen) and the **Whiskey Ring** (officials stealing liquor taxes), discredited Republican government. Reform-minded **Liberal Republicans** broke away in 1872.
- The **Panic of 1873** began a long depression, shifting northern attention to economic problems.
- Many northerners were **weary** of the "southern question" and shared racist assumptions.
- The **Amnesty Act (1872)** restored the right to hold office to most former Confederates.
- The **Supreme Court** narrowed federal protections. In the **Slaughterhouse Cases (1873)**, it read the 14th Amendment's protections narrowly. In **United States v. Cruikshank (1876)**, arising from the Colfax Massacre, it ruled that the 14th Amendment restricted the actions of **states**, not private individuals, weakening federal power to prosecute racial violence.

Congress's final Reconstruction law, the **Civil Rights Act of 1875**, banned racial discrimination in public accommodations like inns and theaters, but it was weakly enforced (and struck down by the Court in 1883).

One by one, Redeemer Democrats retook southern states, often through fraud and intimidation. Under the **Mississippi Plan (1875)**, armed white groups openly threatened Black voters and the federal government did not intervene.

## The Compromise of 1877
The **election of 1876** between Republican **Rutherford B. Hayes** and Democrat **Samuel Tilden** was disputed: Tilden won the popular vote, but the electoral votes of **Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina** — the last southern states with Republican governments — were contested. A special **electoral commission** awarded all the disputed votes to Hayes. In the informal **Compromise of 1877**, southern Democrats accepted Hayes's election; in return, Hayes **withdrew the last federal troops** from the South (and Democrats expected federal aid for southern internal improvements, such as a railroad, and a southerner in the cabinet).

=> Disputed 1876 election; northern retreat from Reconstruction | Compromise of 1877 | Federal troops withdrawn; Redeemer Democrats control the South; Black rights eroded through disfranchisement and segregation (Jim Crow)

Reconstruction was over. Over the following decades, southern states used **poll taxes, literacy tests, and violence** to disfranchise most Black voters and imposed segregation under **Jim Crow** laws. Yet Reconstruction left a permanent legacy: the **14th and 15th Amendments** became the legal basis for the civil rights movement of the twentieth century.

## Beyond the South (1865–1877)
- **Diplomacy:** Secretary of State **William Seward** bought **Alaska** from Russia in **1867** for $7.2 million (mocked as "**Seward's Folly**"). Britain agreed in the **Treaty of Washington (1871)** to arbitrate the **Alabama claims** for damage by British-built Confederate raiders, and later paid $15.5 million.
- **The West:** the **transcontinental railroad** was completed at **Promontory Point, Utah (1869)**, built largely by **Chinese** immigrants (Central Pacific) and **Irish** workers (Union Pacific). Conflict with Plains nations intensified: the **Sand Creek Massacre (1864)** of Cheyenne and Arapaho in Colorado, the **Fort Laramie Treaty (1868)** guaranteeing the Black Hills to the Lakota, and — after gold was found there — the Lakota and Cheyenne victory over **George Custer** at the **Little Bighorn (1876)**.
