## Why this unit matters
The Civil War was the deadliest conflict in American history — roughly **620,000 to 750,000** soldiers died — and it settled two great questions: the Union could not be broken by secession, and **slavery would end**. The exam focuses less on battle tactics than on **why the North won**, **turning points**, **emancipation**, **diplomacy**, and the **home fronts** (draft, finance, wartime laws). Learn about five battles well; learn the policies thoroughly.

## The two sides
After Fort Sumter, eleven states formed the Confederacy. Four slave **border states** — **Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri** — stayed in the Union, and **West Virginia** broke away from Virginia to join the Union (1863). Keeping the border states was vital: Maryland surrounded Washington, D.C., and Kentucky controlled the Ohio River. To hold Maryland, Lincoln **suspended the writ of habeas corpus** there, jailing suspected secessionists without trial — one of several actions that expanded presidential power in wartime. (After the war, in *Ex parte Milligan*, 1866, the Supreme Court ruled that military trials of civilians where civilian courts were open were unconstitutional.)

**Northern advantages:** about **22 million** people vs. **9 million** in the South (about 3.5 million of them enslaved); around **90% of the nation's manufacturing**; most of the **railroads**; a **navy**; an established government and financial system; and more food.

**Southern advantages:** it only had to **defend** and outlast the North, not conquer; it fought on familiar home ground with high morale; and it had excellent generals such as **Robert E. Lee** and **Thomas "Stonewall" Jackson**. Southerners also hoped "**King Cotton**" would force Britain and France to intervene.

**Strategies:** the Union's **Anaconda Plan** (General Winfield Scott) aimed to **blockade** southern ports and **seize the Mississippi River**, squeezing the Confederacy, while also trying to capture the Confederate capital, **Richmond**. The Confederacy fought a mostly **defensive** war, occasionally invading the North (Antietam, Gettysburg) hoping to win foreign recognition and break northern will.

## The early war (1861–1862)
- **First Battle of Bull Run (Manassas), July 1861:** a Confederate victory that shattered northern hopes of a quick war.
- **Monitor vs. Merrimack (Virginia), March 1862:** the first battle between **ironclad** warships, a draw that made wooden navies obsolete.
- In the **West**, General **Ulysses S. Grant** captured Forts Henry and Donelson (1862) and won the bloody **Battle of Shiloh** (April 1862). Admiral **David Farragut** captured **New Orleans** (April 1862).
- In the **East**, General **George McClellan** — a skilled organizer but overly cautious — failed to take Richmond in the **Peninsula Campaign** (1862).

**Antietam (September 17, 1862)** in Maryland was the **bloodiest single day** in American history (about 23,000 casualties). Lee's first invasion of the North was stopped. It was not a clear victory, but it gave Lincoln the opportunity he needed to issue the **preliminary Emancipation Proclamation** — and it discouraged Britain from recognizing the Confederacy. Lincoln later removed McClellan for failing to pursue Lee.

## Emancipation
At first, Lincoln insisted the war was to **preserve the Union**, not to end slavery — partly to keep the border states loyal. ("If I could save the Union without freeing any slave I would do it," he wrote in 1862.) But enslaved people forced the issue by escaping to Union lines. General Benjamin Butler declared such escapees "**contraband of war**" and refused to return them. Congress passed **Confiscation Acts** (1861, 1862) to seize Confederate property, including enslaved people, **abolished slavery in Washington, D.C.** (1862, with compensation to owners), and banned slavery in the territories (1862).

The **Emancipation Proclamation**, effective **January 1, 1863**, declared free all enslaved people in areas **still in rebellion** against the United States. Issued under Lincoln's power as **commander in chief**, it was a **war measure**.

- It did **not** apply to the loyal **border states** or Union-occupied areas — so on the day it took effect it immediately freed few people.
- But it **changed the war's purpose** to include ending slavery; every advance of Union troops now meant freedom.
- It made British or French recognition of the Confederacy politically impossible, since their publics opposed slavery.
- It authorized the enlistment of **Black soldiers**.

=> Union need for manpower, escapes by enslaved people, and the "victory" at Antietam | Emancipation Proclamation (Jan. 1, 1863) | War aims expand to ending slavery; Britain stays out; about 180,000 Black men serve in the Union army

About **180,000 African American soldiers** served in the **US Colored Troops** (about 10% of the Union army), plus thousands in the navy. The **54th Massachusetts** regiment's assault on **Fort Wagner** (1863) proved their courage to doubting whites. Black soldiers served in segregated units under white officers and received **lower pay** until Congress equalized it in 1864. Confederates sometimes executed captured Black soldiers, as at Fort Pillow (1864).

Because the Proclamation was a war measure that might not survive peace, Lincoln pushed for a constitutional amendment. Congress passed the **13th Amendment**, abolishing slavery throughout the United States, in **January 1865**; it was ratified in **December 1865**.

!! Classic exam point: the Emancipation Proclamation freed enslaved people **only in Confederate-held territory**; the **13th Amendment** ended slavery everywhere.

## Turning points: Gettysburg and Vicksburg (July 1863)
After winning at Fredericksburg (December 1862) and Chancellorsville (May 1863, where Stonewall Jackson was accidentally shot by his own men), Lee invaded the North again.

- **Gettysburg (July 1–3, 1863)**, Pennsylvania: General George Meade's Union army repulsed Lee; the disastrous Confederate assault on the third day is known as **Pickett's Charge**. Lee never again invaded the North. In November, Lincoln's **Gettysburg Address** redefined the war as a struggle for "**a new birth of freedom**" and "government of the people, by the people, for the people."
- **Vicksburg (surrendered July 4, 1863)**: after a long siege, Grant captured the last major Confederate stronghold on the Mississippi. The Union now controlled the river, **splitting the Confederacy in two** — fulfilling the Anaconda Plan.

=> Lee's second invasion of the North; Grant's siege of Vicksburg | Union victories at Gettysburg and Vicksburg (July 1863) | Turning point: Confederacy split, Lee on the defensive, foreign intervention no longer likely

## Total war and the end (1864–1865)
Lincoln made **Grant** general-in-chief in 1864. Grant pressed Lee relentlessly in Virginia (the Wilderness, Spotsylvania, Cold Harbor) despite huge losses, then besieged **Petersburg**, the rail hub protecting Richmond. Meanwhile **William Tecumseh Sherman** captured **Atlanta (September 1864)** and then made his **March to the Sea** to Savannah, destroying railroads, crops, and property to break southern civilians' ability and will to fight — **total war**.

The fall of Atlanta helped Lincoln win the **election of 1864** against Democrat **George McClellan**, whose party's platform called the war a failure. Lincoln ran on a "National Union" ticket with **Andrew Johnson**, a pro-Union Democrat from Tennessee, as his running mate.

Lee abandoned Richmond in April 1865 and surrendered to Grant at **Appomattox Court House**, Virginia, on **April 9, 1865**. Grant offered generous terms: Confederate soldiers could go home and keep their horses. Five days later, on **April 14**, **John Wilkes Booth** shot Lincoln at **Ford's Theatre**; he died the next morning, and Andrew Johnson became president.

## Diplomacy
The Confederacy's hope for European recognition rested on **King Cotton diplomacy**. It failed because:
- Britain had a **surplus of cotton** stored from bumper crops before the war and developed new sources in **Egypt and India**.
- Britain needed northern **wheat** ("King Corn") after poor harvests.
- After **Antietam** and the **Emancipation Proclamation**, siding with a slaveholding nation became politically impossible; British workers largely supported the Union.
- US Minister **Charles Francis Adams** skillfully managed relations in London.

Crises still occurred. In the **Trent Affair (1861)**, a US warship stopped a British ship and seized two Confederate diplomats, **Mason and Slidell**; Lincoln released them to avoid war ("one war at a time"). British shipyards built Confederate raiders like the **CSS Alabama**, which sank many Union merchant ships — leading to the postwar "Alabama claims" that Britain paid. France used the war to install **Maximilian** as emperor of **Mexico** (1864), defying the Monroe Doctrine; after the war, US pressure helped force the French out.

## The Union home front
With southern Democrats gone from Congress, Republicans passed long-stalled economic programs:
- **Homestead Act (1862):** **160 acres** of western public land free to settlers who lived on and farmed it for **five years**.
- **Morrill Land-Grant College Act (1862):** federal land to states to fund **agricultural and mechanical colleges**.
- **Pacific Railway Act (1862):** land grants and loans for the **transcontinental railroad** (completed 1869).
- High protective **tariffs** (Morrill Tariff).
- **National Banking Acts (1863–1864):** a system of national banks and a uniform national currency.
- The first federal **income tax** and paper money called **greenbacks** (not backed by gold), plus massive bond sales.

=> Secession removes southern opposition in Congress; war needs revenue | Homestead Act, transcontinental railroad, national banks, tariffs, greenbacks | Expanded federal power over the economy and a boost to northern industry and the West

The Union adopted a **draft** in **1863**. A man could avoid service by hiring a **substitute** or paying a **$300 commutation fee**, leading to complaints of "**a rich man's war and a poor man's fight**." In July 1863, the **New York City draft riots** — largely by Irish immigrant workers who feared competition from freed Black laborers — killed over 100 people, including many African Americans who were lynched.

**Copperheads** (Peace Democrats), such as Ohio's **Clement Vallandigham**, opposed the war and called for a negotiated peace; Vallandigham was arrested and banished to the Confederacy.

**Women** took over farms, shops, and factory jobs, and served as nurses. **Clara Barton** cared for the wounded (she later founded the American Red Cross), **Dorothea Dix** was superintendent of Union army nurses, and the **US Sanitary Commission**, largely run by women volunteers, improved camp hygiene and medical care.

## The Confederate home front
The Confederacy struggled to fight a modern war:
- It passed the first **conscription** law in American history (**1862**), but exempted men who owned **twenty or more enslaved people** — fueling resentment among poor whites.
- It printed huge amounts of paper money, causing **inflation** of roughly 9,000%; food shortages led to **bread riots** (Richmond, 1863).
- Its commitment to **states' rights** hampered President **Jefferson Davis**; some governors resisted central control of troops and supplies.
- The Union **blockade** grew tighter, cutting off imports and cotton exports.
- Late in the war, enslaved people increasingly escaped or slowed work, weakening the South from within. Desperate, the Confederate Congress voted in March 1865 to arm some enslaved men — far too late to matter.

## Results
The war **preserved the Union**, established that states could not secede, **ended slavery**, and greatly **expanded federal power** — including the first national taxes, currency, and draft. The North's economy grew, while the South's was devastated: its railroads, cities, and farms lay in ruins, and its wealth in enslaved people was gone. Reconstruction (Unit 12) would decide what freedom meant.
