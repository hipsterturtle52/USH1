## Why this unit matters
"Antebellum" means "before the war." Between 1820 and 1860, Americans built a remarkable culture of **religious revival**, **reform movements**, and **literature** — while the South built an economy ever more dependent on **slavery**. This unit carries much of the exam's **cultural/intellectual** and **social** weight. Expect matching questions (*reformer → cause*, *writer → work*) and cause-effect questions linking **revivalism to reform**.

## The Second Great Awakening
The **Second Great Awakening** (roughly 1790s–1840s) was a wave of Protestant revivals larger than the first. Frontier **camp meetings** — like the huge gathering at **Cane Ridge, Kentucky (1801)** — drew thousands. The most famous revivalist, **Charles Grandison Finney**, held emotional meetings across western New York, a region swept by so many revivals it was called the "**burned-over district**."

The Awakening's message differed from Puritan predestination. Preachers stressed **free will**: anyone could choose salvation, and believers could work toward **perfection** — personal and social. **Methodists and Baptists** grew fastest. **Women** formed the majority of converts and organized many church and charitable societies.

=> Market revolution and social change; belief in free will and human perfectibility | Second Great Awakening revivals | Wave of reform movements — temperance, abolition, education, asylums, women's rights

The religious ferment also produced new faiths:
- **Mormons** (Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints): **Joseph Smith** published the *Book of Mormon* (1830) in New York. Persecuted for their beliefs and practices (including polygamy), Mormons moved to Ohio, Missouri, and Illinois, where Smith was killed by a mob (1844). **Brigham Young** led them to the Great Salt Lake in **Utah (1847)**.
- **Shakers:** a celibate community that shared property and worshiped through dance; it grew only by conversion.

## Utopian communities
Some reformers tried to build perfect societies in miniature: **Brook Farm** (Massachusetts, linked to transcendentalists), **New Harmony** (Indiana, founded by the Welsh socialist **Robert Owen**), and the **Oneida Community** (New York, John Humphrey Noyes, which practiced "complex marriage" and shared property). Most utopias failed within a few years.

## Transcendentalism and the American Renaissance
**Transcendentalism** was a New England intellectual movement influenced by European **Romanticism**. It held that truth could be found through **intuition, nature, and the individual soul**, "transcending" reason and the senses, and it prized **self-reliance** and **nonconformity**.

- **Ralph Waldo Emerson** — essays like "**Self-Reliance**"; his address "The American Scholar" (1837) called for an independent American intellectual culture.
- **Henry David Thoreau** — **Walden** (1854), about two years of simple living by Walden Pond; and "**Civil Disobedience**" (1849), written after he went to jail for refusing to pay a tax supporting the **Mexican War** and slavery. He argued individuals must refuse to obey unjust laws — an idea that later inspired Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr.
- **Margaret Fuller** — editor of the transcendentalist journal *The Dial*; *Woman in the Nineteenth Century* (1845), an early feminist work.

Other writers of the "**American Renaissance**": **Nathaniel Hawthorne** (*The Scarlet Letter*, a critique of Puritan hypocrisy), **Herman Melville** (*Moby-Dick*), **Edgar Allan Poe** (horror and detective stories), **Walt Whitman** (*Leaves of Grass*, democratic free verse), **James Fenimore Cooper** (frontier novels such as *The Last of the Mohicans*), and **Washington Irving** ("Rip Van Winkle"). In painting, the **Hudson River School** (Thomas Cole) celebrated the grandeur of American landscapes.

!! Romanticism (emotion, nature, the individual) was a reaction against the Enlightenment's emphasis on reason. Transcendentalism is the American version.

## The reform impulse
- **Temperance:** Americans drank enormous amounts of alcohol, which reformers linked to poverty, crime, and domestic abuse. The **American Temperance Society (1826)** urged abstinence; **Maine** passed the first statewide **prohibition** law (1851).
- **Public education:** **Horace Mann**, secretary of the **Massachusetts Board of Education** (1837), led the **common school** movement for free, tax-supported public schools with trained teachers and longer school years. **McGuffey Readers** taught reading and moral values.
- **Asylums and prisons:** **Dorothea Dix** documented the brutal treatment of the **mentally ill** in jails and persuaded states to build **mental hospitals**. Reformers also built **penitentiaries** meant to rehabilitate criminals through discipline and reflection.

## Women: separate spheres and women's rights
The market revolution moved paid work out of the home. Middle-class culture embraced the "**cult of domesticity**" (or "**separate spheres**"): men belonged in the competitive public world; women were to be pious, pure, and devoted to the home as moral guardians. Ironically, this moral role justified women's activism in **reform** — temperance, charity, and abolition.

Working in the abolition movement, women discovered their own lack of rights; at the 1840 World Anti-Slavery Convention in London, female delegates were not allowed to participate. In **1848**, **Elizabeth Cady Stanton** and **Lucretia Mott** organized the **Seneca Falls Convention** in New York. Its **Declaration of Sentiments**, modeled on the Declaration of Independence, declared that "all men **and women** are created equal" and listed grievances — including the demand for the **right to vote**, the most controversial resolution. Other leaders included **Susan B. Anthony** (who joined in the 1850s), the **Grimké sisters** (southern-born abolitionists), and **Sojourner Truth**, a formerly enslaved woman known for her speech "Ain't I a Woman?" (1851).

=> Women's work in reform movements exposes their own lack of rights | Seneca Falls Convention (1848) and the Declaration of Sentiments | Organized women's rights movement begins; suffrage becomes a long-term goal

## Abolitionism
Early antislavery efforts were cautious. The **American Colonization Society (1816)** proposed freeing enslaved people gradually and sending free Black Americans to Africa; it founded **Liberia**. Most African Americans rejected colonization — they considered America their home.

In the 1830s, abolitionism became radical:
- **William Lloyd Garrison** began publishing **The Liberator (1831)**, demanding **immediate, uncompensated emancipation**. He called the Constitution "a covenant with death" because it protected slavery. He co-founded the **American Anti-Slavery Society (1833)**.
- **Frederick Douglass**, who escaped slavery in Maryland, became the movement's most powerful speaker; he wrote the **Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass** (1845) and published the newspaper **The North Star**. Unlike Garrison, he came to see the Constitution as an antislavery tool and supported political action.
- **David Walker's Appeal (1829)**, by a free Black Bostonian, urged enslaved people to resist, by force if necessary.
- **Harriet Tubman** escaped slavery and returned repeatedly to guide others north on the **Underground Railroad**, a secret network of safe houses.
- Political abolitionists formed the **Liberty Party (1840)**.

Abolitionists were a **small and unpopular minority**, even in the North. Mobs attacked them; the editor **Elijah Lovejoy** was killed in Illinois (1837). Southern states banned abolitionist mail, and from 1836 to 1844 the House of Representatives enforced a **gag rule** that automatically tabled antislavery petitions — opposed relentlessly by former president **John Quincy Adams**, then a congressman.

## The slave South and "King Cotton"
After the cotton gin, cotton spread across the Deep South — Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas. By 1860, cotton made up more than half of US exports, and southerners boasted that "**Cotton is King**." The enslaved population grew from about 1.5 million (1820) to about **4 million (1860)**, mostly by natural increase, since the international trade was banned in 1808. A massive **domestic slave trade** forcibly moved roughly a million people from the Upper South to the cotton states, tearing families apart.

White southern society was a pyramid:
- A small **planter elite** (owners of 20 or more enslaved people) dominated politics.
- Most slaveholders owned only a few enslaved people — yet because large plantations held so many people, about half of all enslaved people lived on holdings of 20 or more.
- About **three-quarters of white southern families owned no enslaved people at all**; most were **yeoman farmers**, who nevertheless generally supported slavery because of racial attitudes, hopes of becoming slaveholders, and kinship ties.
- **Free Black southerners** (about 250,000) faced severe legal restrictions.

The South industrialized little and built fewer railroads and schools than the North; its wealth was tied up in land and enslaved people.

Enslaved people built a resilient culture: extended **families** (though marriages had no legal standing), **Christianity** blended with African traditions, **spirituals** that often carried hidden messages of freedom, and folktales. They resisted through work slowdowns, sabotage, escape, and occasionally revolt. **Denmark Vesey's** planned rebellion in Charleston (1822) was betrayed. **Nat Turner's Rebellion (1831)** in Virginia killed about 60 whites; in response, southern states passed **harsher slave codes**, restricted education and assembly of enslaved people, and ended open debate about gradual emancipation.

=> Nat Turner's Rebellion (1831) and rising northern abolitionism | Southern crackdown: harsher slave codes, censorship of abolitionist mail | The South shifts from calling slavery a "necessary evil" to defending it as a "positive good"

Southern defenders like **John C. Calhoun** argued slavery was a "**positive good**" that civilized and cared for enslaved people, and **George Fitzhugh** claimed enslaved people were better off than northern "**wage slaves**."

## Immigration and nativism
Immigration surged in the 1840s–1850s. **Irish** immigrants, fleeing the **potato famine** (1845–1849), were mostly poor **Catholics** who settled in eastern cities, took low-paying jobs (canals, railroads, domestic service), and usually voted **Democratic**. **Germans**, including many fleeing the failed revolutions of 1848, often had more money and moved to Midwestern farms and cities. Most immigrants avoided the South, which had few factory jobs.

Native-born Protestants responded with **nativism** — hostility to immigrants and Catholics. The secretive Order of the Star-Spangled Banner grew into the **Know-Nothing (American) Party** in the 1850s, which sought to limit immigrant political power.

Workers also organized. In **Commonwealth v. Hunt (1842)**, the Massachusetts Supreme Court ruled that **labor unions were not illegal** conspiracies.
