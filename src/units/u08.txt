## Why this unit matters
**Andrew Jackson** dominated an age that historians call the era of the "**common man**." Politics became more democratic for white men, a new **two-party system** (Democrats vs. Whigs) took shape, and Jackson used presidential power aggressively — against the **Bank**, against **South Carolina's nullification**, and against **Native nations** through **Indian Removal**. The exam tests both sides of Jacksonian democracy: expanding participation for some, exclusion and removal for others.

## A more democratic politics — for white men
Between about 1810 and 1840, most states dropped **property requirements** for voting, achieving near-universal **white male suffrage**. Several other changes made politics more popular:
- Presidential electors came to be chosen by **popular vote** in nearly every state rather than by legislatures.
- **National nominating conventions** replaced the "King Caucus" of congressmen; the small **Anti-Masonic Party** held the first one in 1831.
- Campaigns used parades, barbecues, slogans, and cheap **penny newspapers**; voter turnout rose dramatically.

But democracy had limits. Several states that expanded white male voting **took the vote away from free Black men** (Pennsylvania in 1838, for example). Women could not vote anywhere, and Native peoples were being expelled.

!! A frequent exam trap: Jacksonian democracy expanded voting for **white men only**. Answers claiming it broadened rights for women, free Black Americans, or Native peoples are wrong.

## The election of 1828 and Jackson's style
After the "corrupt bargain" of 1824, Jackson's supporters built a new party — the **Democrats** — organized largely by **Martin Van Buren**. The **1828** campaign was famously nasty (opponents attacked Jackson's marriage), and Jackson won easily, the first president from the West and not from Virginia or Massachusetts. Crowds mobbed the White House at his inauguration.

Jackson believed ordinary citizens could hold government jobs and that long-serving officials grew corrupt. He defended "**rotation in office**," and his supporters' rewarding of loyal party workers with jobs became known as the **spoils system** ("to the victor belong the spoils"). He relied on informal advisers, his "**Kitchen Cabinet**," and used the **veto** more than all previous presidents combined — often for political, not just constitutional, reasons.

## Indian Removal
By the 1820s, the "**Five Civilized Tribes**" of the Southeast — **Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole** — held land that white settlers and cotton planters wanted. The Cherokee in particular had adopted many white customs: a written language (created by **Sequoyah**), a newspaper, a constitution, and farms (some even owned enslaved people). Georgia nevertheless claimed authority over Cherokee land, especially after gold was found there.

Jackson pushed the **Indian Removal Act (1830)**, which funded treaties to move eastern Native nations to "**Indian Territory**" west of the Mississippi (present-day Oklahoma).

The Cherokee fought in court:
- **Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831):** the Supreme Court called the Cherokee a "**domestic dependent nation**" and said it could not hear the case.
- **Worcester v. Georgia (1832):** Chief Justice **Marshall** ruled that Georgia's laws had **no force** in Cherokee territory; only the federal government could deal with the Cherokee.

Jackson **refused to enforce** the ruling (he is quoted, possibly apocryphally, as saying "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it"). A small minority signed the **Treaty of New Echota (1835)**, which the Cherokee majority rejected. In **1838**, under President Van Buren, the army forced about 16,000 Cherokee west on the **Trail of Tears**; roughly **4,000** died. Other nations were removed too. The **Seminole** resisted in Florida in the long **Second Seminole War** (1835–1842), led partly by **Osceola**; in the Midwest, the Sauk and Fox were defeated in the **Black Hawk War (1832)**.

=> Settler demand for land and cotton; Georgia claims Cherokee territory | Indian Removal Act (1830), Worcester v. Georgia ignored, Trail of Tears (1838) | Southeastern nations forced west to Indian Territory; thousands die; cotton kingdom expands

## The nullification crisis
The **Tariff of 1828** — the "**Tariff of Abominations**" — had very high rates protecting northern manufacturers. South Carolina, suffering from falling cotton prices and worn-out soil, blamed the tariff, which raised prices of goods southerners bought and risked British retaliation against cotton.

Vice President **John C. Calhoun** of South Carolina anonymously wrote the **South Carolina Exposition and Protest (1828)**, arguing — like the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions — that a state could **nullify** (declare void within its borders) a federal law it judged unconstitutional.

In the **Webster–Hayne debate (1830)** in the Senate, **Daniel Webster** of Massachusetts answered South Carolina's Robert Hayne with a defense of national union: "**Liberty and Union, now and forever, one and inseparable!**" At a dinner, Jackson made his position clear with a toast: "Our Federal Union — it must be preserved." Calhoun replied, "The Union, next to our liberty, most dear."

When Congress passed the **Tariff of 1832** with only modest reductions, South Carolina passed an **Ordinance of Nullification** declaring the tariffs void and threatening **secession** if the federal government used force. Jackson, though a southerner and slaveholder, called nullification treason and got Congress to pass the **Force Bill (1833)**, authorizing him to use the military to collect tariffs. At the same time, Henry Clay arranged the **Compromise Tariff of 1833**, gradually lowering rates over ten years. South Carolina repealed its nullification of the tariff (and symbolically nullified the Force Bill). Calhoun **resigned** the vice presidency to lead the states' rights cause in the Senate.

=> High protective tariffs hurt South Carolina's cotton economy | Nullification crisis (1832–1833) | Compromise Tariff + Force Bill; Union preserved, but the states' rights / secession argument grew stronger in the South

## The Bank War
Jackson distrusted banks, paper money, and concentrated wealth. He saw the **Second Bank of the United States**, headed by **Nicholas Biddle**, as an unconstitutional "**monster**" that served eastern elites and foreign investors at the expense of farmers and workers.

The Bank's charter ran until 1836, but in **1832** Henry Clay and Biddle pushed an **early recharter** bill through Congress to make it an election issue. Jackson **vetoed** it, calling the Bank unconstitutional (despite *McCulloch*) and a tool of privilege. Jackson won the **1832 election** overwhelmingly over Clay.

Jackson then "killed" the Bank by **removing federal deposits** and placing them in state banks loyal to the administration — called "**pet banks**." His Treasury Secretary **Roger Taney** carried out the removal (Jackson had to fire two secretaries who refused); Jackson later made Taney Chief Justice.

The consequences were serious. Pet banks issued paper money freely, fueling **land speculation** and inflation. Alarmed, Jackson issued the **Specie Circular (1836)**, requiring that federal land be bought with **gold or silver (specie)**. Confidence collapsed, and the **Panic of 1837** struck just as **Martin Van Buren** took office, bringing a long depression. Van Buren responded with the **Independent Treasury** (1840), keeping federal money in government vaults rather than banks.

=> Jackson views the Bank as an unconstitutional monopoly of the rich | Bank veto (1832), removal of deposits to pet banks, Specie Circular (1836) | Speculation, then the Panic of 1837 and depression under Van Buren

## The second party system: Democrats vs. Whigs
Opponents of "**King Andrew I**" formed the **Whig Party** in 1834 (named after the British party that opposed royal power). The two parties competed nationally, in both North and South:

- **Democrats** (Jackson, Van Buren): limited federal government, opposition to the national bank and high tariffs, suspicion of concentrated economic power, support for westward expansion. Strong among small farmers, southern planters, urban workers, and **Irish Catholic immigrants**.
- **Whigs** (Henry Clay, Daniel Webster): an active federal government promoting economic growth through Clay's **American System** — tariffs, a national bank, internal improvements — and a strong **Congress** over the presidency. Strong among businessmen, northern merchants, many evangelical **Protestant reformers**, and some southern planters.

In the **election of 1840**, Whigs beat Van Buren, blamed for the depression, by copying Democratic tactics. They ran war hero **William Henry Harrison** as a humble man of the people with a "**log cabin and hard cider**" campaign and the slogan "**Tippecanoe and Tyler Too**" — even though Harrison came from a wealthy Virginia family. Turnout reached about 80%. Harrison died a month after his inauguration, and Vice President **John Tyler**, a former Democrat and states' rights southerner, vetoed most Whig bills, including a new national bank. His own party expelled him.

Tyler's secretary of state, Daniel Webster, negotiated the **Webster–Ashburton Treaty (1842)** with Britain, settling the **Maine–Canada border**.

## The Taney Court
Chief Justice **Roger Taney** (1836–1864) moved the Court away from Marshall's protections of existing corporate privileges. In **Charles River Bridge v. Warren Bridge (1837)**, the Court ruled that a charter did not give an old bridge company a monopoly when the community would benefit from a new bridge — favoring **competition and the public interest** over exclusive privileges. Taney is best known, however, for the **Dred Scott** decision (Unit 10).

## Assessing Jackson
Jackson expanded **presidential power**, strengthened party politics, and championed the "common man" against privilege. But his era also produced Indian Removal, the defense of slavery, and economic instability. On exams, expect to weigh both sides.
