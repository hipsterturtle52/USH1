## Why this unit matters
This is one of the heaviest units on the exam. In 25 years the new nation created a working government, split into the first **political parties**, doubled its size with the **Louisiana Purchase**, established **judicial review**, and fought Britain in the **War of 1812**. The organizing theme: **Hamilton vs. Jefferson** — two visions of America that shaped every debate. Learn each president's major events and how the parties lined up.

## Washington sets precedents (1789–1797)
**George Washington** knew everything he did would become a model. He created a **cabinet** of advisers: **Thomas Jefferson** (State), **Alexander Hamilton** (Treasury), **Henry Knox** (War), and **Edmund Randolph** (Attorney General). He served **two terms** and then retired, a precedent that lasted until 1940. The **Judiciary Act of 1789** created the federal court system below the Supreme Court.

Congress, led by **James Madison**, passed the **Bill of Rights**, ratified in **1791** to fulfill the Federalists' promise. Highlights: the **1st Amendment** (religion, speech, press, assembly, petition), the **2nd** (bear arms), the **4th** (no unreasonable searches), the **5th** (due process, no self-incrimination), and the **10th** (powers not given to the national government are **reserved to the states** or the people).

## Hamilton's financial plan
**Alexander Hamilton** wanted a strong national government tied to the wealthy, with a modern economy of commerce and manufacturing. His program:
1. **Funding at par:** pay the national debt in full to current bondholders (many were speculators who had bought bonds cheap).
2. **Assumption:** the federal government would take over **state war debts**. Southern states, which had largely paid their debts, objected. In the **Compromise of 1790**, southerners accepted assumption in exchange for locating the **national capital on the Potomac River** (Washington, D.C.).
3. A **Bank of the United States (1791)**, chartered for 20 years, to hold federal funds, issue currency, and make loans.
4. **Tariffs** and an **excise tax on whiskey** to raise revenue. His *Report on Manufactures* proposed protective tariffs to build industry, but Congress largely did not adopt it.

The Bank sparked the first great constitutional debate. **Jefferson** argued for **strict construction**: the Constitution did not list a power to charter a bank, so Congress could not. **Hamilton** argued for **loose construction**: the **"necessary and proper" (elastic) clause** gave Congress **implied powers** to carry out its listed powers, like collecting taxes and borrowing money. Washington sided with Hamilton.

=> Hamilton's plan to build national credit | Bank of the United States (1791) | Debate over strict vs. loose construction; rise of opposing political parties

## The first party system
Though Washington warned against "faction," two parties formed:

- **Federalists** (Hamilton, John Adams): strong central government, **loose construction**, commerce and manufacturing, the Bank, rule by the educated elite, **pro-British** in foreign policy. Strongest in **New England** and among merchants.
- **Democratic-Republicans** (Jefferson, Madison): limited government, **strict construction**, **states' rights**, an **agrarian** nation of independent farmers, **pro-French**. Strongest in the **South and West**.

!! Expect a question that matches a belief to the right party — especially *strict vs. loose construction* and *pro-French vs. pro-British*.

## Testing the government at home and abroad
The **Whiskey Rebellion (1794)**: western Pennsylvania farmers, who turned grain into whiskey to transport it, refused to pay Hamilton's excise tax and attacked tax collectors. Washington led about **13,000 militiamen** west, and the rebellion collapsed. Unlike Shays' Rebellion under the Articles, the new government had shown it could **enforce federal law**.

Abroad, the **French Revolution** (1789) and the war between France and Britain (1793) divided Americans. Washington issued the **Proclamation of Neutrality (1793)**, keeping the US out despite the 1778 French alliance. The French envoy **Citizen Genêt** tried to recruit Americans to attack British and Spanish territory and was recalled.

- **Jay's Treaty (1794)** with Britain: the British agreed to leave their **forts in the Northwest**, but did nothing about the seizure of American ships or **impressment** (forcing sailors into the Royal Navy). It was very **unpopular**, but it avoided war.
- **Pinckney's Treaty (1795)** with Spain: gave Americans free navigation of the **Mississippi River** and the **right of deposit** at **New Orleans**, and settled Florida's border. It was very **popular**, especially in the West.
- In the Northwest, General **Anthony Wayne** defeated a Native confederacy at the **Battle of Fallen Timbers (1794)**; in the **Treaty of Greenville (1795)**, Native nations ceded most of present-day **Ohio**.

In his **Farewell Address (1796)**, Washington warned against **permanent alliances** with foreign nations, against the "baneful effects" of **political parties**, and against **sectionalism**. The advice to avoid entangling alliances guided US foreign policy for over a century.

## John Adams (1797–1801)
Federalist **John Adams** inherited conflict with France, which was seizing American ships. In the **XYZ Affair (1797–1798)**, French agents (labeled X, Y, and Z) demanded a **bribe** before negotiating with American diplomats. Americans were outraged ("Millions for defense, but not one cent for tribute!") and fought an undeclared naval **Quasi-War** with France. Adams eventually negotiated peace in the **Convention of 1800**, ending the 1778 alliance — a decision that split his own party.

Federalists used the war scare to pass the **Alien and Sedition Acts (1798)**:
- The **Alien Acts** raised the residency requirement for citizenship (from 5 to 14 years) and let the president deport dangerous foreigners — aimed at immigrants, who tended to vote Democratic-Republican.
- The **Sedition Act** made it a crime to publish "false, scandalous, and malicious" writing against the government — used to jail Republican newspaper editors.

Jefferson and Madison responded secretly with the **Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (1798)**, arguing the Union was a **compact** among states and that states could judge federal laws unconstitutional (Kentucky's later version used the word **nullification**). No other states agreed at the time, but the idea of **states' rights** and nullification would return in the 1830s and in secession.

=> War scare with France and Federalist fear of Republican critics | Alien and Sedition Acts (1798) | Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions assert states' rights; Federalists lose popularity

## The "Revolution of 1800"
In the **election of 1800**, Jefferson defeated Adams. Because electors cast two undifferentiated votes, Jefferson and his running mate **Aaron Burr** **tied**, and the House of Representatives chose Jefferson (with Hamilton's help). The **12th Amendment (1804)** fixed this by having electors vote separately for president and vice president. Jefferson called it the "**Revolution of 1800**" because power passed **peacefully** from one party to its rival — a rare event in the world at the time.

## Jefferson in power (1801–1809)
Jefferson cut the national debt, the army, and the navy, and repealed the whiskey tax — but kept the Bank. Before leaving office, Adams had appointed Federalist "**midnight judges**." One appointee, William Marbury, sued when Jefferson's secretary of state James Madison withheld his commission.

In **Marbury v. Madison (1803)**, Chief Justice **John Marshall** ruled that the law giving the Court power to issue Marbury's commission was itself **unconstitutional**. Marbury lost, but the Court won something far bigger: the power of **judicial review** — the authority to declare acts of Congress unconstitutional.

!! Marbury v. Madison = judicial review. It is the single most-tested Supreme Court case of this era.

The **Louisiana Purchase (1803)** was Jefferson's greatest achievement. France's **Napoleon**, having lost his army in the **Haitian Revolution** and needing money for war in Europe, sold the entire Louisiana Territory for about **$15 million**, **doubling** the nation's size. Jefferson had only wanted New Orleans. As a **strict constructionist**, he doubted the Constitution allowed buying land, but he justified it under the **treaty-making power** — a sign that practical needs could override his principles. **Lewis and Clark** (1804–1806), guided in part by the Shoshone woman **Sacagawea**, explored the territory to the Pacific.

=> Slave revolt in Haiti defeats French troops; Napoleon needs money for war | Louisiana Purchase (1803) | US doubles in size; Mississippi and New Orleans secured; Jefferson bends strict construction

Jefferson also sent the navy against the **Barbary pirates** of Tripoli (1801–1805) rather than keep paying tribute.

## Neutral rights and the road to war
Britain and France were at war again, and both seized American ships. Britain also practiced **impressment**, taking thousands of American sailors. In the **Chesapeake affair (1807)**, a British warship fired on an American naval vessel and took sailors off it. Instead of war, Jefferson persuaded Congress to pass the **Embargo Act (1807)**, banning all American exports. It was a disaster: it hurt American merchants and farmers — especially **New England** — far more than Britain or France. It was repealed in 1809 and replaced by the **Non-Intercourse Act**, which reopened trade with all nations except Britain and France.

## James Madison and the War of 1812
Under President **James Madison**, pressure for war grew from the **War Hawks** — young congressmen from the South and West such as **Henry Clay** and **John C. Calhoun**. Causes of the war:
- **Impressment** and violations of **neutral rights** at sea.
- British support for Native resistance in the Northwest. The Shawnee leader **Tecumseh** and his brother the Prophet (**Tenskwatawa**) built a pan-Indian confederacy to stop American expansion; **William Henry Harrison** attacked their town in the **Battle of Tippecanoe (1811)**.
- Western desire for land, including hopes of conquering **Canada**.

Congress declared war in **June 1812**. American invasions of Canada failed. **Oliver Hazard Perry** won control of **Lake Erie (1813)**, and Tecumseh was killed at the **Battle of the Thames (1813)**, ending his confederacy. In **1814** the British **burned Washington, D.C.**, but failed to take Baltimore; the defense of **Fort McHenry** inspired **Francis Scott Key's** "The Star-Spangled Banner."

New England Federalists, who opposed "Mr. Madison's War," met at the **Hartford Convention (1814–1815)** and proposed constitutional amendments to limit southern and western power (some discussed secession). The timing was terrible: news arrived of peace and victory, and the Federalists looked **disloyal**. The party soon **collapsed**.

The **Treaty of Ghent** (signed **December 1814**) restored prewar conditions (*status quo ante bellum*) — no territory changed hands and impressment was not mentioned. Two weeks later, before news crossed the ocean, **Andrew Jackson** crushed the British at the **Battle of New Orleans (January 1815)**, making him a national hero.

=> Impressment, neutral rights, and British aid to Tecumseh | War of 1812 | Surge of nationalism, death of the Federalist Party, Native resistance east of the Mississippi broken, and growth of American manufacturing

## Society in the new republic
- **Republican Motherhood:** the idea that women served the nation by raising virtuous, educated citizen-sons; it justified better **education for women**, though not political rights.
- **Slavery:** **Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793)** made short-staple cotton profitable and revived slavery just as some had hoped it would fade. **Gabriel's Rebellion (1800)**, a planned uprising near Richmond, was betrayed and crushed. Congress banned the **international slave trade** as of **1808**, the earliest date the Constitution allowed.
- **Culture:** a new national identity appeared in schoolbooks, Noah Webster's American spelling, and the celebration of Washington.
