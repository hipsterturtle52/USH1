## Why this unit matters
After the War of 1812, Americans felt a surge of **nationalism**. Government policies, Supreme Court decisions, and foreign policy all pointed toward a stronger, more unified nation. At the same time, a **market revolution** — canals, steamboats, factories, and commercial farming — transformed how Americans worked and lived. But beneath the "Era of Good Feelings," **sectional** conflict over slavery surfaced in the **Missouri Crisis**. The exam tests the **American System**, **Marshall Court cases**, the **Monroe Doctrine**, the **Missouri Compromise**, and the **Erie Canal and Lowell mills**.

## The Era of Good Feelings
With the Federalist Party dead after the Hartford Convention, the Democratic-Republicans governed alone. President **James Monroe** (1817–1825) won reelection in 1820 essentially unopposed. A Boston newspaper called this one-party period the "**Era of Good Feelings**." The name is a bit misleading: personal and **sectional rivalries** remained, and the **Panic of 1819** brought real suffering.

## Clay's American System
The war had exposed weaknesses: poor roads, dependence on British goods, and financial chaos after the first Bank's charter lapsed in 1811. Even Republicans now adopted former Federalist ideas. Speaker of the House **Henry Clay** of Kentucky promoted the **American System**, a plan to tie the regions together economically:
1. A **protective tariff** to shield northern industry from cheaper British goods. The **Tariff of 1816** was the first tariff designed mainly for **protection** rather than revenue.
2. A **Second Bank of the United States** (chartered **1816**) to provide a stable national currency and credit.
3. Federally funded **internal improvements** — roads and canals — to connect western farms with eastern markets.

=> War of 1812 exposes economic weakness; nationalist mood | Clay's American System (tariff, national bank, internal improvements) | Economic integration of regions — but disputes over whether the federal government could fund roads and canals

Tariffs and the Bank passed, but federal funding for internal improvements stalled: President Madison vetoed the **Bonus Bill (1817)**, believing it needed a constitutional amendment. Most canals and roads were built by **states and private companies** instead. The federally funded **National (Cumberland) Road** was the major exception.

The **Panic of 1819**, the nation's first major depression, followed a boom in land speculation and easy credit. When the Second Bank tightened credit, banks failed and farmers lost land. Many westerners came to **hate the Bank** — feelings Andrew Jackson would exploit.

## The Marshall Court strengthens the nation
Chief Justice **John Marshall** (served 1801–1835) issued rulings that strengthened the **federal government** and protected **property and business**:

- **McCulloch v. Maryland (1819):** Maryland tried to tax the Second Bank. Marshall ruled that the Bank was constitutional under **implied powers** (the necessary and proper clause — Hamilton's loose construction) and that a state could not tax a federal institution because "**the power to tax involves the power to destroy**." Federal law is supreme over state law.
- **Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819):** New Hampshire could not alter Dartmouth's colonial charter, because a charter is a **contract** protected by the Constitution — encouraging business investment.
- **Gibbons v. Ogden (1824):** a New York steamboat monopoly was void because only **Congress** may regulate **interstate commerce**, broadly defined.
- Earlier, **Fletcher v. Peck (1810)** was the first time the Court struck down a **state** law as unconstitutional (also protecting contracts).

!! Pattern to remember: Marshall's Court almost always ruled for **national power over the states** and for **contracts and property**. If an answer choice says the Marshall Court strengthened states' rights, it is almost certainly wrong.

## Nationalist foreign policy
Secretary of State **John Quincy Adams** was one of the most skillful diplomats in American history.

- **Rush–Bagot Agreement (1817):** the US and Britain demilitarized the **Great Lakes**.
- **Convention of 1818:** set the US–Canada border at the **49th parallel** from Minnesota to the Rocky Mountains and agreed to **joint occupation of Oregon**.
- **Adams–Onís Treaty (1819):** after **Andrew Jackson** invaded Spanish **Florida** chasing Seminoles (the First Seminole War), Adams persuaded Spain to **cede Florida** and define the western border of the Louisiana Purchase. Spain gave up its claim to Oregon.

The **Monroe Doctrine (1823)**, written largely by Adams, responded to newly independent **Latin American** republics and fears that European powers (and Russia in the Pacific Northwest) would intervene. It declared:
- The Western Hemisphere was **closed to further European colonization**.
- The US would **not interfere in European affairs** or existing colonies.
- European intervention in the Americas would be seen as a threat to the US.

=> Latin American revolutions; fear of European intervention | Monroe Doctrine (1823) | Assertion of US leadership in the hemisphere — though at first enforced mainly by Britain's navy

At the time the US had little power to enforce the Doctrine; Britain, which wanted open Latin American markets, effectively enforced it with its navy. Britain had proposed a joint statement, but Adams insisted the US act **alone**.

## The Missouri Compromise
In 1819, **Missouri** asked to join the Union as a slave state. That would upset the balance of **11 free and 11 slave states** in the Senate. New York Congressman **James Tallmadge** proposed banning further importation of enslaved people into Missouri and gradually freeing children born there; the House passed it, but the Senate blocked it. Henry Clay brokered the **Missouri Compromise (1820)**:
- **Missouri** entered as a **slave state**.
- **Maine** (split off from Massachusetts) entered as a **free state**, keeping the Senate balanced.
- Slavery was **banned in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase north of 36°30′** (Missouri's southern border).

The aging Thomas Jefferson called the crisis "**a fire bell in the night**," warning that the slavery issue could destroy the Union. The compromise held for over 30 years — until the Kansas–Nebraska Act (Unit 10) repealed it.

=> Missouri's request to enter as a slave state threatens the Senate balance | Missouri Compromise (1820) | Temporary sectional peace; 36°30′ line set the rule for slavery in the Louisiana Territory until 1854

## The market revolution: transportation
Before 1815, moving goods overland was slow and expensive; most farm families produced mainly for their own use. The **transportation revolution** changed that.

- **Roads:** turnpikes and the **National Road** (begun 1811) linked the Potomac to the Ohio River.
- **Steamboats:** **Robert Fulton's** *Clermont* (1807) proved steam power could carry goods **upstream**; steamboats soon dominated the Mississippi and Ohio Rivers.
- **Canals:** New York's **Erie Canal**, completed in **1825** under Governor **DeWitt Clinton**, linked the **Hudson River to the Great Lakes**. It slashed shipping costs, opened the Midwest to settlement, and made **New York City** the nation's dominant port. A canal-building craze followed.
- **Railroads:** the **Baltimore and Ohio** began operating around 1830; by the 1850s railroads surpassed canals and tied the Northeast to the Midwest.
- **Communication:** Samuel **Morse's telegraph** (1844) sent messages instantly.

## The market revolution: industry and agriculture
**Samuel Slater**, a British mechanic who memorized textile machine designs, built America's first successful water-powered **cotton-spinning mill** in Rhode Island (1790s) — earning the title "father of the American factory system."

In Massachusetts, **Francis Cabot Lowell** and the Boston Associates went further, putting all stages of cloth production under one roof (Waltham, 1814) and building the factory town of **Lowell** in the 1820s. The **Lowell system** hired young, unmarried **farm women** ("mill girls"), housed them in supervised **boardinghouses**, and paid them cash wages. Many valued the independence, but long hours and wage cuts led to some of the first **strikes** by women workers in the 1830s. Later, Irish immigrants replaced them.

**Eli Whitney** also promoted **interchangeable parts** — standardized components that made mass production possible.

Western farmers became commercial producers thanks to new tools: **John Deere's steel plow** (1837) broke the tough prairie soil, and **Cyrus McCormick's mechanical reaper** (1830s) harvested grain quickly.

## Results of the market revolution
- **Regional specialization:** the **Northeast** focused on manufacturing and commerce; the **South** on cotton grown by enslaved people; the **West (Midwest)** on grain and livestock. The regions became economically interdependent — and the Northeast and Midwest increasingly bound together by canals and rails, leaving the South apart.
- Families shifted from **subsistence** to **market** production, buying goods they once made.
- A **middle class** grew, and work moved out of the home. This created the idea of "**separate spheres**": men in the public world of work, women in the private home (Unit 9).
- Workers lost independence as artisans were replaced by **wage labor**; boom-and-bust cycles like the Panic of 1819 hit hard.

## The election of 1824 and the "corrupt bargain"
By 1824 the one-party era fractured. Four Democratic-Republicans ran: **Andrew Jackson**, **John Quincy Adams**, **William Crawford**, and **Henry Clay**. Jackson won the most popular and electoral votes but **not a majority**, so the **House of Representatives** chose among the top three. Clay, as Speaker, threw his support to Adams, who won — and then named Clay **Secretary of State**. Jackson's supporters denounced this "**corrupt bargain**."

President **John Quincy Adams** (1825–1829) proposed an ambitious nationalist program — roads, canals, a national university, scientific research — but Congress, dominated by Jackson's supporters, blocked most of it. The **Tariff of 1828** (the "**Tariff of Abominations**"), with very high rates, enraged the South and set up the nullification crisis of Unit 8.
