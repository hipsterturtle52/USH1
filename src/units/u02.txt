## Why this unit matters
In less than a century, England planted colonies from Maine to the Carolinas. The exam tests the **contrasts between regions** — the Chesapeake, New England, the Middle colonies, and the Carolinas — and the moments that shaped them: **Jamestown**, the **Mayflower Compact**, **Puritan dissenters**, **Bacon's Rebellion**, and **King Philip's War**. Learn each region as a package: *who came, why, what they grew, and how they governed*.

## The Chesapeake: Virginia and Maryland
**Jamestown (1607)** was founded by the **Virginia Company**, a joint-stock company hoping for gold. The first years were a disaster: colonists chose a swampy site, many were gentlemen unused to labor, and disease and hunger killed most of them. **John Smith** imposed discipline ("he that will not work shall not eat"), and the colony survived only with food from the **Powhatan Confederacy**. The winter of 1609–1610 was the **"starving time."**

=> Search for profit; no gold found | John Rolfe's tobacco (1612) becomes a cash crop | Demand for land and labor, war with the Powhatans, and a boom economy

**Tobacco** saved Virginia. **John Rolfe** introduced a sweet strain around 1612 (he also married **Pocahontas**, daughter of Powhatan, bringing a brief peace). Tobacco needed vast land and many workers. Virginia attracted labor with the **headright system**: anyone who paid a person's passage received 50 acres per head. Most early laborers were **indentured servants** — mostly poor young Englishmen who worked 4–7 years in return for passage and "freedom dues."

**1619** was a landmark year: the **House of Burgesses**, the first elected representative assembly in English America, met; and the first recorded **Africans** arrived in Virginia. After a Powhatan attack in 1622, the king revoked the Virginia Company's charter and made Virginia a **royal colony (1624)**.

**Maryland (1634)** was a **proprietary colony** granted to **Lord Baltimore** (the Calvert family) as a refuge for English **Catholics**. Because Protestants soon outnumbered Catholics, Maryland passed the **Act of Toleration (1649)**, protecting all *Christians* (not non-Christians) — an early but limited step toward religious freedom.

Chesapeake society was rough: heavily male, high death rates from malaria, scattered tobacco plantations along rivers, few towns, and weak churches and schools.

## Bacon's Rebellion and the turn to slavery
By the 1670s, many freed servants were landless and angry. Rich planters held the best land near the coast, and Governor **William Berkeley** refused to attack Native peoples on the frontier (he profited from the fur trade). In **1676**, **Nathaniel Bacon** led frontiersmen and servants in **Bacon's Rebellion**, attacking Native villages and burning **Jamestown**. The revolt collapsed when Bacon died of disease.

=> Landless freedmen, falling tobacco prices, frontier conflict with Native peoples | Bacon's Rebellion (1676) | Planters turn from indentured servants to enslaved Africans, who could not become free and rebellious landless men

Planters concluded that freed servants were dangerous. As English wages improved and fewer servants came, and as the **Royal African Company** supplied more captives, the Chesapeake shifted to **African slavery**. Virginia's laws hardened: a 1662 law made a child's status follow the **mother** (so children of enslaved women were enslaved), and later **slave codes** made slavery lifelong, hereditary, and racial.

## New England: Pilgrims and Puritans
New England was founded for **religious** reasons. Two groups matter:

- **Pilgrims** were **Separatists** — they believed the Church of England was so corrupt they had to leave it entirely. Sailing on the *Mayflower*, they founded **Plymouth (1620)**. Before landing they signed the **Mayflower Compact**, an agreement to form a "civil body politic" and obey laws made for the general good — an early example of **self-government by consent**. With help from **Squanto** (Tisquantum) and the Wampanoag leader **Massasoit**, they survived.
- **Puritans** wanted to **purify** the Church of England from within (non-separatists). Led by **John Winthrop**, they founded the **Massachusetts Bay Colony (1630)**, part of the **Great Migration** of about 20,000 people in the 1630s. Winthrop told them they would be "**a city upon a hill**" — a model Christian community watched by the world.

Puritans believed in **predestination** (God already chose the saved, the "elect") and the **covenant**: a community bound to God and each other. Only church members (the "visible saints") could vote in the colony. Puritans valued literacy so people could read the Bible: they founded **Harvard (1636)** and passed laws requiring towns to support schools.

New England grew differently from the Chesapeake. Puritans came as **families**, the climate was healthier, and people lived longer. Settlement was organized around compact **towns** with a church and a **town meeting**. The rocky soil supported small **subsistence farms**, so the economy turned to **fishing, lumber, shipbuilding, and trade**.

## Dissent and new colonies
Puritans wanted religious freedom for themselves, not for others. Dissenters were expelled:

- **Roger Williams** argued for **separation of church and state** and that colonists should buy land fairly from Native peoples. Banished, he founded **Rhode Island (1636)**, which offered broad religious toleration.
- **Anne Hutchinson** claimed believers could receive direct revelation from God and that ministers were stressing good works (a view called **antinomianism**). She was tried and banished in 1638 — a challenge both to Puritan theology and to expectations of women.
- **Thomas Hooker** led settlers to **Connecticut**; its **Fundamental Orders (1639)** are often called the first written constitution in America.

As fewer children of Puritans had a conversion experience, churches adopted the **Half-Way Covenant (1662)**, allowing their children to be baptized without full membership — a sign of declining religious zeal. The **Salem witch trials (1692)**, in which 20 people were executed, reflected social tensions, fear, and anxiety in late-1600s Massachusetts.

## Conflict with Native peoples
Expansion brought war. The **Pequot War (1637)** ended with a massacre of Pequots at Mystic. The bloodiest conflict was **King Philip's War (1675–1676)**, led by the Wampanoag leader **Metacom** (called King Philip by the English). Native forces destroyed many frontier towns, but disease, food shortages, and Mohawk attacks defeated them. Afterward, Native power in southern New England was broken.

=> English settlers pressing onto Native land and pressure to adopt English ways | King Philip's War (1675–1676) | Native resistance in southern New England crushed; colonists' hostility toward Native peoples hardened

## The Middle colonies
After 1660, the restored king **Charles II** granted land to his supporters (the "Restoration colonies").

- **New York:** the English seized Dutch **New Netherland** in **1664** and renamed it for the **Duke of York**. It kept a diverse population — Dutch, English, Jews, Africans — and New York City became a trading hub.
- **Pennsylvania (1681):** **William Penn**, a **Quaker**, founded a "**holy experiment**." Quakers believed in an **inner light** in every person, pacifism, and equality (women could preach). Pennsylvania offered **religious toleration**, a representative assembly, and fair purchase of land from the **Lenape** (Delaware) — Philadelphia became the fastest-growing colonial city.
- **New Jersey** and **Delaware** completed the region.

The Middle colonies were the most **ethnically and religiously diverse**. Fertile soil made them the "**breadbasket**" colonies, exporting wheat and flour.

## The Carolinas
**Carolina (1663)** was granted to eight proprietors. Its southern part was settled by planters from **Barbados**, an English sugar island where slavery was harsh and dominant. They brought the West Indian slave model with them; later, **rice** cultivation (relying on the skills of West Africans) made **South Carolina** a majority-enslaved colony by the early 1700s. **Charleston** was its port. North Carolina, poorer and more independent, had small tobacco farms.

## Governing the empire
Colonies were **royal** (crown-run), **proprietary** (owned by an individual), or **corporate/charter** (self-governing, like Connecticut and Rhode Island). England briefly tried to tighten control: James II combined the northern colonies into the **Dominion of New England (1686)** under **Edmund Andros**, abolishing assemblies. When James II was overthrown in England's **Glorious Revolution (1688–1689)**, colonists ousted Andros and the Dominion collapsed — a precedent for resisting arbitrary royal power.

## Big comparison
- **Chesapeake:** profit, tobacco, servants then slaves, men, scattered plantations, high death rate.
- **New England:** religion, families, towns, town meetings, diverse small-scale economy, long lives.
- **Middle:** diversity, toleration, grain, trade cities.
- **Carolinas:** Barbados model, rice, slave majority.
