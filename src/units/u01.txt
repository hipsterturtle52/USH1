## Why this unit matters
The exam opens where American history opens: millions of people already living in the Americas, and Europeans arriving with new motives and new tools. Expect questions on **Native American diversity**, **why Europeans explored**, the **Columbian Exchange**, and how **Spain, France, and England** built very different empires. This unit is only about 5% of the exam, so focus on the big contrasts, not every explorer.

## The Americas before 1492
Before Europeans arrived, the Americas held somewhere between 50 and 100 million people (estimates vary widely). They were not one culture but hundreds, shaped by environment.

- **Mesoamerica and the Andes:** the **Maya**, **Aztecs** (capital Tenochtitlán, in today's Mexico City), and **Inca** (Andes Mountains) built large empires with cities, roads, tribute systems, and advanced astronomy. **Maize (corn)** cultivation was the foundation that made large populations possible.
- **Southwest:** the **Ancestral Puebloans** (Anasazi) and later **Pueblo** peoples farmed corn with irrigation in a dry land and built multistory adobe and cliff dwellings.
- **Mississippi River valley:** the **Mississippian culture** built mound cities. **Cahokia** (near today's St. Louis) may have held 10,000–20,000 people around 1100 CE.
- **Northeast woodlands:** the **Iroquois Confederacy** (Haudenosaunee) united five nations — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca — under the Great Law of Peace. Many eastern peoples combined farming (the "Three Sisters": corn, beans, squash) with hunting. In many of these societies, women farmed and held real political influence; Iroquois clan mothers helped choose leaders.
- **Great Plains and Great Basin:** more mobile hunting and gathering. (The horse-centered Plains culture came *later*, after Spanish horses spread north.)
- **Pacific Northwest:** abundant salmon allowed settled villages without farming; the **potlatch** was a gift-giving ceremony that displayed status.

!! The exam likes the idea that Native societies adapted to their environment, and that most Native peoples viewed land as something used in common, not owned as private property. That difference fueled centuries of conflict.

## Why Europe went exploring
Europe in the 1400s was changing fast. Historians often summarize motives as **"God, gold, and glory."**

=> Ottoman control of eastern trade routes and demand for Asian spices | Search for an all-water route to Asia | Portuguese voyages around Africa and Columbus sailing west

- **Gold:** Europeans wanted Asian spices, silks, and gold without paying Muslim and Italian middlemen.
- **God:** the **Reconquista** — Spain's centuries-long campaign to drive Muslim rulers out of Iberia — ended in **1492** with the fall of Granada, leaving Spain's monarchs, **Ferdinand and Isabella**, eager to spread Catholicism. Later, the **Protestant Reformation** (begun by **Martin Luther** in **1517**) turned Catholic–Protestant rivalry into a reason to compete overseas.
- **Glory:** new centralized **nation-states** (Portugal, Spain, France, England) could fund expeditions and wanted prestige.
- **Tools:** the **Renaissance** spread curiosity and learning; the **printing press** spread maps and travel accounts; the **caravel** ship, the **compass**, and the **astrolabe** made long voyages possible.

**Portugal** led first. **Prince Henry the Navigator** sponsored voyages down Africa's coast. **Bartolomeu Dias** rounded the Cape of Good Hope (1488) and **Vasco da Gama** reached India (1498). Portugal also began the Atlantic trade in enslaved Africans to work sugar plantations on Atlantic islands — a model later copied in the Americas.

## Columbus and the Spanish empire
**Christopher Columbus**, an Italian sailing for Spain, reached the Caribbean in **1492**, believing he was near Asia. Spain and Portugal quickly divided the non-European world in the **Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)**, which is why Brazil speaks Portuguese and most of Latin America speaks Spanish.

Spanish **conquistadores** followed. **Hernán Cortés** conquered the Aztec Empire (1519–1521) and **Francisco Pizarro** the Inca (1530s). They won with small forces because of **disease** (smallpox killed huge numbers), Native allies who hated Aztec rule, steel weapons, and horses. Silver from mines like **Potosí** made Spain the richest power in Europe for a century.

Spain controlled labor through the **encomienda system**, which granted colonists the labor and tribute of Native people in return for "protecting" and Christianizing them — in practice, near-slavery. The Dominican friar **Bartolomé de Las Casas** denounced these abuses in his writings. Spain's colonies became a rigid, **hierarchical society** in which Spanish-born *peninsulares* ranked above American-born *creoles*, *mestizos* (mixed Spanish and Native ancestry), Native people, and enslaved Africans. Spanish men frequently intermarried with Native women, unlike most later English colonists.

In what is now the United States, Spain founded **St. Augustine, Florida (1565)** — the oldest continuously occupied European city in the US — and pushed into New Mexico under **Juan de Oñate** (1598). Catholic **missions** tried to convert Pueblo peoples while suppressing their religion and demanding labor. In the **Pueblo Revolt of 1680**, a leader named **Popé** united the Pueblos and drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for about twelve years — the most successful Native uprising against European colonization. When Spain returned, it allowed more tolerance of Pueblo customs.

## The Columbian Exchange
The **Columbian Exchange** was the transfer of plants, animals, people, and diseases between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492. It is one of the most tested ideas in this unit.

- **Americas → Europe/Africa/Asia:** maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cacao, tobacco. American crops fed Europe's population growth.
- **Europe/Africa → Americas:** horses, cattle, pigs, wheat, sugarcane, and — most important — **diseases** such as **smallpox** and measles, to which Native peoples had no immunity. Epidemics killed a large majority of the Native population in many regions.
- **People:** Europeans and, increasingly, enslaved Africans brought to replace dying Native laborers.

=> Native peoples had no immunity to Old World diseases | Smallpox and other epidemics spread ahead of colonizers | Native population collapse made conquest easier and drove demand for enslaved African labor

## France and the Netherlands
The French came for **fur, fish, and souls**, not gold. **Jacques Cartier** explored the St. Lawrence River (1530s), and **Samuel de Champlain** founded **Quebec (1608)**. New France stayed thinly populated; the French relied on **alliances and trade with Native peoples** (especially the Huron), intermarried, and sent **Jesuit missionaries** who lived among Native communities. Because they needed Native partners rather than land, the French generally had better relations with Native peoples than the English did.

The Dutch, sailing with **Henry Hudson** (1609), built a fur-trading colony, **New Netherland**, with its capital **New Amsterdam** on Manhattan (more in Unit 2).

## England's late start
England was slower. **John Cabot** claimed Newfoundland for England in **1497**, but England was absorbed in religious upheaval after **Henry VIII** broke with the pope (1530s) and founded the Church of England. Under **Elizabeth I**, English "sea dogs" like **Francis Drake** raided Spanish treasure ships.

The defeat of the **Spanish Armada in 1588** was a turning point: it weakened Spanish sea power and opened North America to English colonization.

England's first attempt, **Roanoke** (Sir Walter Raleigh's colony, 1585–1587), failed; the 1587 colonists vanished — the "**Lost Colony**." Writers like **Richard Hakluyt** urged colonization to rival Spain, spread Protestantism, and employ England's "surplus" poor.

Why did English people eventually come in large numbers? At home, the **enclosure movement** pushed farmers off land, the population grew, and inheritance by **primogeniture** (eldest son inherits) left younger sons looking for opportunity. New **joint-stock companies** let investors pool money and share risk — the tool that would fund Jamestown in 1607.

=> Spanish Armada defeated; overpopulation and enclosure at home; joint-stock companies | England turns to colonization | Jamestown (1607) and the English colonies of Unit 2

## Big comparisons to remember
- **Spain:** conquest, gold and silver, missions, encomienda, intermarriage, rigid hierarchy.
- **France:** fur trade, few settlers, Native alliances, Jesuits.
- **England:** many settlers, farming families, took land, kept Native peoples separate — the subject of the rest of this course.

Q S1 Which of the following best explains the dramatic decline of Native American populations after 1492?
+ Epidemic diseases such as smallpox to which Native peoples had no immunity || Disease killed far more Native people than warfare or enslavement; it often spread ahead of the Europeans themselves.
- Large-scale warfare among Native nations over European trade goods || Intertribal war did occur, but it was not the main cause of the population collapse.
- Mass emigration of Native peoples to Asia across the Bering Strait || There was no such reverse migration; the Bering land bridge migration happened thousands of years earlier.
- Famine caused by the introduction of maize from Europe || Maize was native to the Americas and went the other way, to Europe.
- A deliberate Spanish policy of extermination ordered by the Treaty of Tordesillas || The Treaty of Tordesillas divided territory between Spain and Portugal; it said nothing about extermination.

- leading the conquest of the Inca Empire || That was Francisco Pizarro.
