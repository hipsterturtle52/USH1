## Why this unit matters
This is the **most heavily weighted unit**. The story is one long chain: **expansion** added new land → every new territory reopened the question **"Will slavery be allowed there?"** → each attempted compromise failed → the parties broke apart → **secession**. If you can explain each link in that chain, you can answer most questions in this unit. Learn the compromises and laws **in order** and what each did about slavery in the territories.

## Manifest Destiny
In 1845 the editor **John L. O'Sullivan** wrote that it was America's "**manifest destiny** to overspread the continent allotted by Providence." The phrase captured a belief that the United States was **destined by God** to expand to the Pacific, spreading democracy and Protestant civilization. Motives included land hunger, Pacific ports for Asian trade, fear of British influence, and — for many southerners — new land for **slavery and cotton**. The idea also carried assumptions of racial superiority over Mexicans and Native peoples.

## Texas
After **Mexico** won independence from Spain (1821), it invited Americans to settle Texas; **empresarios** like **Stephen F. Austin** brought in settlers who agreed to become Mexican citizens and Catholics. By 1830 Americans (many with enslaved people) far outnumbered Mexicans in Texas. Tension grew when Mexico **abolished slavery (1829)**, restricted further American immigration, and **General Antonio López de Santa Anna** centralized power.

In the **Texas Revolution (1835–1836)**, Santa Anna wiped out the defenders of the **Alamo** in San Antonio (March 1836) — "Remember the Alamo!" — but **Sam Houston's** army defeated and captured him at **San Jacinto** (April 1836). Texas became the independent **Lone Star Republic** and asked to join the US. Presidents Jackson and Van Buren held back: annexing Texas would add a huge **slave state** and risk **war with Mexico**, which never recognized Texas's independence.

## Oregon and the election of 1844
Thousands of families followed the **Oregon Trail** in the 1840s to the fertile Willamette Valley, which the US and Britain jointly occupied. In the **election of 1844**, the Democrats nominated a "dark horse," **James K. Polk** of Tennessee, on a platform of expansion: the "**reoccupation of Oregon and the reannexation of Texas**." Some Democrats demanded all of Oregon up to 54°40′ ("**Fifty-four forty or fight!**"). Polk beat Henry Clay. Interpreting the vote as a mandate, outgoing President Tyler got Congress to annex **Texas by joint resolution (1845)** (a joint resolution needed only a majority in each house, not the two-thirds Senate vote a treaty required).

Polk then compromised with Britain: the **Oregon Treaty (1846)** extended the border along the **49th parallel** to the Pacific. Polk avoided war with Britain because he was preparing for war with Mexico.

## The Mexican–American War (1846–1848)
The US and Mexico disputed Texas's southern border: Texans claimed the **Rio Grande**, Mexico the **Nueces River** farther north. Polk also wanted **California**, but Mexico refused to sell. Polk sent General **Zachary Taylor** into the disputed zone. When Mexican troops attacked an American patrol there, Polk told Congress that Mexico had "shed **American blood upon American soil**," and Congress declared war (May 1846).

Many **Whigs and abolitionists** opposed the war as a land grab for slavery. A first-term Whig congressman, **Abraham Lincoln**, introduced "**spot resolutions**" demanding Polk identify the exact spot where American blood was shed. Thoreau went to jail rather than pay taxes supporting the war.

The US won quickly: Taylor won in northern Mexico; **Stephen Kearny** took New Mexico; Americans in California revolted (the **Bear Flag Revolt**); and **Winfield Scott** captured **Mexico City** (1847).

The **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848)** gave the US the **Mexican Cession** — California, Nevada, Utah, and most of Arizona and New Mexico — and confirmed the **Rio Grande** border of Texas. The US paid **$15 million** and assumed Americans' claims against Mexico. In the **Gadsden Purchase (1853)**, the US bought a strip of southern Arizona and New Mexico for a southern railroad route.

=> Manifest Destiny; Polk's desire for California; disputed Texas border | Mexican–American War (1846–1848) | Huge Mexican Cession — and an explosive question: would slavery be allowed in the new land?

## The question of slavery in the territories
In **1846**, Pennsylvania Congressman **David Wilmot** proposed the **Wilmot Proviso**: slavery would be **banned in any land acquired from Mexico**. It passed the House (where the North had more seats) repeatedly but **failed in the Senate**. Votes split along **sectional**, not party, lines — a dangerous sign.

Four positions emerged:
1. **Free soil:** Congress should ban slavery in all territories (Wilmot Proviso). Many free-soilers wanted the West for **white farmers and free labor**, not necessarily equality for Black people.
2. **Extend the Missouri Compromise line (36°30′)** to the Pacific.
3. **Popular sovereignty:** let the **settlers of each territory vote** on slavery (Senator **Lewis Cass**, later **Stephen Douglas**). Appealing because it seemed democratic and kept Congress out of it — but vague about *when* settlers could decide.
4. **Calhoun's view:** slaves were property protected by the Constitution, so **neither Congress nor territorial governments could ban slavery** in any territory.

In **1848**, antislavery Democrats, Whigs, and Liberty Party men formed the **Free Soil Party** ("Free Soil, Free Speech, Free Labor, and Free Men"), nominating **Martin Van Buren**. Whig war hero **Zachary Taylor** won.

## The Compromise of 1850
The **California Gold Rush (1849)** brought about 100,000 "forty-niners," and California quickly applied to enter the Union as a **free state** — which would break the 15–15 balance of free and slave states in the Senate. Southerners threatened secession. In the Senate's last great debate, **Henry Clay** proposed a compromise, **Daniel Webster** supported it in his "Seventh of March" speech ("I wish to speak today, not as a Massachusetts man, nor as a Northern man, but as an American"), and the dying **John C. Calhoun** opposed it. President Taylor opposed it, but he died in July 1850; President **Millard Fillmore** supported it, and **Stephen Douglas** passed it as separate bills.

The **Compromise of 1850**:
- **California** admitted as a **free state** (for the North).
- **New Mexico and Utah** territories organized with **popular sovereignty** on slavery (for the South).
- **Slave trade (not slavery) banned in Washington, D.C.** (for the North).
- A much stronger **Fugitive Slave Act** (for the South).
- Texas gave up land claims in New Mexico in exchange for federal payment of its debts.

=> California's application as a free state threatens the Senate balance | Compromise of 1850 | Brief calm — but the new Fugitive Slave Act radicalizes many northerners

## The Fugitive Slave Act and Uncle Tom's Cabin
The **Fugitive Slave Act** denied accused runaways a **jury trial** or the right to testify, paid federal commissioners **$10** if they returned a person to slavery but only **$5** if they freed them, and required northern citizens to **assist** in captures. Free Black northerners were kidnapped into slavery. Northerners who had ignored slavery now saw it enforced on their streets. Crowds tried to rescue captives, and several states passed **personal liberty laws** to obstruct the Act. In **1854**, it took federal troops to return **Anthony Burns** from Boston to slavery.

**Harriet Beecher Stowe's** novel **Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852)**, written in response to the Act, portrayed slavery's cruelty — especially the breakup of families — and sold hundreds of thousands of copies. It turned many northerners against slavery and outraged southerners, who called it lies.

## The Kansas–Nebraska Act and Bleeding Kansas
Senator **Stephen Douglas** of Illinois wanted a **transcontinental railroad** on a northern route through Chicago, which required organizing the land west of Missouri. To win southern votes, his **Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854)** created two territories, Kansas and Nebraska, to decide slavery by **popular sovereignty** — which **repealed the Missouri Compromise** line, since both lay north of 36°30′.

=> Douglas wants a northern railroad route and southern votes | Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854) repeals the Missouri Compromise | Bleeding Kansas, death of the Whig Party, birth of the Republican Party

Northerners were furious. In Kansas, proslavery and antislavery settlers rushed in to win the vote. Thousands of "**border ruffians**" from Missouri crossed over to vote illegally, electing a proslavery legislature; free-staters formed a rival government. Violence followed — **Bleeding Kansas**: proslavery men sacked the free-state town of **Lawrence** (1856), and the abolitionist **John Brown** led the murder of five proslavery settlers at **Pottawatomie Creek**. Violence reached Congress: after Massachusetts Senator **Charles Sumner** gave his "Crime Against Kansas" speech, South Carolina Congressman **Preston Brooks** beat him nearly to death with a cane on the Senate floor (1856). Southerners sent Brooks new canes; northerners saw southern brutality.

## The party system collapses
The **Whig Party** split over Kansas–Nebraska and disappeared. In **1854**, opponents of the Act founded the **Republican Party**, a purely **northern** party committed to stopping the **expansion** of slavery into the territories (not abolishing it where it existed). Republicans celebrated "**free labor**" — the idea that a man could work for wages, save, and become an independent farmer or businessman, which they believed slavery degraded.

In **1856**, Republican **John C. Frémont** carried most northern states; Democrat **James Buchanan** won, with ex-president Fillmore running for the Know-Nothings. The Democrats were now the only truly national party.

## Dred Scott and Lecompton
**Dred Scott**, an enslaved man, sued for freedom because his owner had taken him to live in free Illinois and the free Wisconsin Territory. In **Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)**, Chief Justice **Roger Taney** ruled that:
- Black people, enslaved or free, were **not citizens** and could not sue in federal court;
- Congress had **no power to ban slavery in the territories**, because enslaved people were property protected by the **Fifth Amendment** — so the **Missouri Compromise had been unconstitutional**.

The decision thrilled the South and enraged the North: it seemed to make slavery legal in every territory and destroyed the Republicans' core program — and even undercut popular sovereignty.

In Kansas, the proslavery minority wrote the **Lecompton Constitution** (1857). Buchanan backed it; Stephen Douglas opposed it as a fraud on popular sovereignty, splitting the Democrats. Kansas voters rejected it, and Kansas entered as a free state in 1861. The **Panic of 1857** hit the North harder than the cotton South, convincing southerners that "King Cotton" made them strong.

## Lincoln–Douglas debates and Harpers Ferry
In **1858**, Abraham Lincoln challenged Douglas for his Illinois Senate seat. Accepting the nomination, Lincoln declared: "**A house divided against itself cannot stand.** I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free." In the **Lincoln–Douglas debates**, Lincoln pressed Douglas on how popular sovereignty could survive Dred Scott. In the **Freeport Doctrine**, Douglas answered that territories could still keep slavery out by refusing to pass the local laws (slave codes) needed to protect it. Douglas kept his Senate seat but **lost southern support**; Lincoln became nationally known.

In **October 1859**, John Brown led about 20 men in a raid on the federal arsenal at **Harpers Ferry**, Virginia, hoping to arm enslaved people for a revolt. He was captured and **hanged**. Many northerners mourned him as a martyr; southerners concluded the North wanted to incite slave rebellion.

## The election of 1860 and secession
In **1860**, the **Democratic Party split**: northern Democrats nominated **Douglas** (popular sovereignty), and southern Democrats nominated **John C. Breckinridge** (federal protection of slavery in all territories). The new **Constitutional Union Party** nominated **John Bell**, who simply urged preserving the Union. The Republicans nominated **Lincoln** on a platform opposing the **expansion** of slavery, plus a protective tariff, a **homestead** law, and a transcontinental railroad.

Lincoln won with about **40% of the popular vote** — every northern free state's electoral votes and **none from the South** (he was not even on the ballot in most southern states).

=> Lincoln, a Republican opposed to slavery's expansion, wins without any southern electoral votes | Secession of the Deep South (Dec. 1860–Feb. 1861) | Confederate States of America formed; crisis over federal forts leads to war

**South Carolina** seceded in **December 1860**, followed by six more Deep South states by February 1861. They formed the **Confederate States of America**, with **Jefferson Davis** as president. Secessionists said they were protecting their rights and property; their declarations stated plainly that the protection of **slavery** was the central cause.

Senator **John J. Crittenden** of Kentucky proposed the **Crittenden Compromise**, which would have extended the **36°30′ line to the Pacific** and protected slavery south of it with constitutional amendments. Lincoln **rejected** any extension of slavery into the territories, and the plan died.

In his **inaugural address (March 1861)**, Lincoln said he had no intention of interfering with slavery where it existed, declared secession illegal, and pledged to hold federal property: "We are not enemies, but friends." When he sent supplies to **Fort Sumter** in Charleston Harbor, Confederate batteries opened fire on **April 12, 1861**. The fort surrendered; Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers; and **Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and Arkansas** then seceded. The Civil War had begun.
