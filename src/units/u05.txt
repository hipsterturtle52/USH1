## Why this unit matters
Having rejected a powerful king and Parliament, Americans first built a **deliberately weak** national government — the **Articles of Confederation**. Its failures led to the **Constitution of 1787**. The exam tests the **weaknesses of the Articles**, its one big success (the **Northwest Ordinance**), **Shays' Rebellion**, the **compromises** of the Constitutional Convention, and the **Federalist vs. Anti-Federalist** debate. Think of it as a story: *fear of tyranny → too-weak government → crisis → stronger but limited government*.

## State constitutions first
After 1776, each state wrote a **written constitution** — a new idea, since Britain's constitution was unwritten. Most included a **bill of rights**, made the **legislature** the strongest branch, and kept **governors weak**, reflecting distrust of executive power after George III. Massachusetts (1780) pioneered having a special **convention** write the constitution and the people ratify it — the model later used for the federal Constitution.

## The Articles of Confederation (ratified 1781)
Drafted in 1777, the **Articles of Confederation** created a "**firm league of friendship**" among sovereign states. Ratification was delayed until **1781** because **Maryland** refused to sign until states with western land claims (like Virginia) gave those lands to the national government.

How it worked:
- A **one-house (unicameral) Congress**; each state had **one vote**, regardless of population.
- **No executive** (no president) and **no national courts**.
- Major laws needed **9 of 13** states; amendments needed **all 13** (unanimity).
- Congress could make war and treaties, borrow money, and run a postal system.
- But Congress **could not tax** (it could only request money from the states), **could not regulate interstate or foreign commerce**, and **could not force states** to obey.

=> Fear of centralized tyranny after the Revolution | Articles create a weak league of sovereign states | National government cannot tax, pay debts, regulate trade, or enforce its laws

## The great success: western lands
Congress handled the western lands well — its most lasting achievement.

- **Land Ordinance of 1785:** surveyed the Northwest Territory into **townships** six miles square, each divided into 36 sections; one section in each township was reserved to support **public schools**. Land sales were meant to pay the national debt.
- **Northwest Ordinance of 1787:** created a government for the territory north of the Ohio River and set a path to **statehood on equal footing** with the original states (when a territory reached 60,000 free inhabitants). It **banned slavery** in the Northwest Territory, protected religious freedom and trial by jury, and encouraged education.

!! The Northwest Ordinance is a classic test item: *equal statehood* + *ban on slavery north of the Ohio River* + *the Articles' greatest achievement*.

## Troubles of the 1780s
- **Money and debt:** Congress could not pay soldiers or foreign loans; states printed paper money that lost value.
- **Trade:** states taxed one another's goods; Britain closed its West Indies ports to American ships.
- **Foreign weakness:** Britain kept **forts in the Northwest** despite the peace treaty; **Spain closed the Mississippi River** and New Orleans to American trade; **Barbary pirates** of North Africa seized American ships.

The crisis that shocked leaders was **Shays' Rebellion (1786–1787)**. In western Massachusetts, farmers — many Revolutionary veterans — faced high state taxes, debts, and the **foreclosure** of their farms. Led by **Daniel Shays**, they closed courts to stop foreclosures and marched on the federal arsenal at Springfield. The national government **could not respond**; Massachusetts merchants funded a state militia that crushed the rebellion.

=> Debt, high state taxes, and farm foreclosures | Shays' Rebellion (1786–1787) | Leaders fear disorder and call for a stronger national government at the Philadelphia Convention

A meeting on trade at the **Annapolis Convention (1786)** drew only five states, but **Alexander Hamilton** and **James Madison** used it to call for a broader convention in Philadelphia in 1787.

## The Constitutional Convention (1787)
Fifty-five delegates met in Philadelphia from May to September 1787, with **George Washington** presiding. **James Madison** did so much of the planning that he is called the "**Father of the Constitution**." They quickly decided to replace the Articles rather than revise them, and worked in secret.

The key debates ended in compromises:

- **Representation:** The **Virginia Plan** (Madison; favored large states) proposed a two-house legislature with representation by **population**. The **New Jersey Plan** (favored small states) kept one house with **equal** votes per state. The **Great (Connecticut) Compromise**, proposed by **Roger Sherman**, created a **House of Representatives** based on population and a **Senate** with **two senators per state**.
- **Slavery and representation:** The **Three-Fifths Compromise** counted three-fifths of the enslaved population for both representation and direct taxes — boosting southern power in the House and Electoral College.
- **Slave trade:** Congress could not ban the **international slave trade before 1808** (it did so in 1808). A **fugitive slave clause** required the return of escaped enslaved people. The word "slavery" never appears.
- **Commerce:** Congress could regulate interstate and foreign trade, but could **not tax exports** (a concession to southern exporters).
- **Choosing a president:** the **Electoral College**, a compromise between election by Congress and by direct popular vote.

## Principles of the Constitution
- **Popular sovereignty:** power comes from the people ("We the People").
- **Federalism:** power divided between national and state governments; the **supremacy clause** makes the Constitution and federal laws the "supreme law of the land."
- **Separation of powers** among legislative, executive, and judicial branches, with **checks and balances** (veto, veto override, Senate confirmation, impeachment).
- **Limited government** and an **amendment process** (two-thirds of Congress, three-fourths of states).
- Congress gained the powers the Articles lacked: to **tax**, **regulate commerce**, and make laws "**necessary and proper**" for its powers.

## Ratification: Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists
The Constitution required approval by **9 of 13** state conventions. A fierce debate followed.

- **Federalists** supported the Constitution. Hamilton, Madison, and John Jay wrote **The Federalist Papers** (85 essays) to win over New York. In **Federalist No. 10**, Madison argued that a **large republic** would control **factions** because so many interests would check each other. Federalists tended to be merchants, urban dwellers, and the well-educated.
- **Anti-Federalists** — including **Patrick Henry** and **George Mason** — feared the strong central government would crush the states and individual liberty, that a president might become a king, and above all that the Constitution lacked a **bill of rights**. They were often small farmers and backcountry people.

=> Anti-Federalist fear that the Constitution lacked protection for individual liberties | Federalists promise to add amendments | Bill of Rights (ratified 1791)

**New Hampshire** became the ninth state in June **1788**, putting the Constitution into effect, but the large states of **Virginia** and **New York** ratified only narrowly after Federalists promised a bill of rights. North Carolina and Rhode Island joined after the new government began. **George Washington** was inaugurated in **1789**, and Madison drafted the **Bill of Rights** — the first ten amendments, ratified in **1791** (covered in Unit 6).
