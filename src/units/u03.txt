## Why this unit matters
Between about 1680 and 1763 the colonies grew from struggling outposts into prosperous, crowded, increasingly self-confident societies. This unit explains the **economic system** that bound them to Britain (**mercantilism**), the spread of **slavery**, two big intellectual currents (**the Great Awakening** and **the Enlightenment**), the habit of **self-government** that grew under **salutary neglect**, and the **French and Indian War** that changed everything. Unit 4's revolution grows directly out of this unit.

## Mercantilism and the Navigation Acts
**Mercantilism** was the economic theory behind the British Empire: a nation grows rich by exporting more than it imports and piling up gold and silver. Colonies existed to benefit the **mother country** — supplying raw materials (tobacco, rice, lumber, furs) and buying finished British goods.

Parliament enforced this through the **Navigation Acts** (beginning in 1651 and 1660). Key rules:
- Colonial trade had to use **English or colonial ships**.
- Certain "**enumerated goods**" — like tobacco, sugar, and later rice — could be shipped **only to England**.
- Most European goods bound for the colonies had to pass through England first.

=> Mercantilist belief that colonies should enrich the mother country | Navigation Acts restrict colonial trade | Colonial resentment and widespread smuggling; later British enforcement after 1763 sparks protest

In practice, the system worked tolerably well for colonists: they had a protected market, and British naval protection. Many merchants simply **smuggled** to avoid duties, especially molasses from the French West Indies (the **Molasses Act of 1733** taxed it but was rarely enforced).

## Salutary neglect and colonial self-government
From about the 1720s to 1763, Britain — under Prime Minister **Robert Walpole** — followed a policy later called **salutary neglect**: it loosely enforced trade laws and let colonies run their own affairs as long as they stayed loyal and profitable. Edmund Burke later praised this as a wise "neglect."

During these decades **colonial assemblies** grew powerful. Royal governors were appointed in London, but assemblies controlled the **"power of the purse"** — taxes and even the governor's salary. Colonists came to see their assemblies as small parliaments and believed only their own elected representatives could tax them. Voting was limited to property-owning white men, but because land was widespread, a much larger share of men could vote than in England.

The **Zenger trial (1735)** advanced freedom of the press: New York printer **John Peter Zenger** was acquitted of libel for criticizing the governor because what he printed was true.

!! Salutary neglect matters because it explains *why* colonists reacted so strongly after 1763: they had grown used to self-government, and new British taxes felt like a break from long tradition.

## The Atlantic economy and slavery
Colonial trade formed a web often called the **triangular trade**: for example, New England rum went to Africa to buy enslaved people; enslaved Africans were carried to the West Indies and mainland colonies; and sugar and molasses went back to New England to make more rum.

The voyage of captives across the Atlantic was the **Middle Passage** — weeks chained below decks, with very high death rates. Roughly 10–12 million Africans were carried across the Atlantic over the whole slave trade; most went to the Caribbean and Brazil, and roughly 400,000 to mainland North America.

By the 1700s, slavery existed in **every colony**, but it was the base of the southern economy: **tobacco** in the Chesapeake, **rice and indigo** in South Carolina and Georgia. In South Carolina enslaved people outnumbered whites. Unlike Caribbean populations, the enslaved population of North America grew by **natural increase** after the early 1700s, and enslaved people built families, churches, and a distinctive culture blending African traditions.

Enslaved people resisted — by working slowly, running away, and occasionally by revolt. In the **Stono Rebellion (1739)** in South Carolina, about 60–100 enslaved people marched toward Spanish Florida, which promised freedom; the revolt was crushed and South Carolina passed a harsher **slave code**. **Slave codes** across the colonies denied enslaved people the right to assemble, travel, own weapons, or learn to read.

**Georgia (1732)**, the last of the thirteen colonies, was founded by **James Oglethorpe** as a **buffer** against Spanish Florida and a fresh start for the "worthy poor" (including some former debtors). It originally **banned slavery** and large landholdings, but settlers protested and slavery was legalized in **1751**.

## A growing, diverse population
Colonial population roughly doubled every 25 years, from about 250,000 in 1700 to 2.5 million by 1775, through high birth rates and immigration. Newcomers included the **Scots-Irish** (Presbyterians who moved to the western frontier and clashed with Native peoples) and **Germans** (often called the "Pennsylvania Dutch"), many of whom settled in Pennsylvania's fertile backcountry.

Society was hierarchical: a wealthy **gentry** of planters and merchants at the top, many small farmers in the middle, and servants and the enslaved at the bottom. Women under the legal doctrine of **coverture** lost their separate legal identity when they married — their property and earnings belonged to their husbands.

## The Great Awakening
The **Great Awakening** (1730s–1740s) was a wave of emotional religious revivals. Preachers stressed personal conversion, sin, and an emotional relationship with God rather than formal learning.

- **Jonathan Edwards**, a Massachusetts minister, preached "**Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God**" (1741), warning listeners of hell.
- **George Whitefield**, an English evangelist, toured the colonies drawing huge outdoor crowds; even the skeptical Ben Franklin admired his speaking.

Churches split between "**New Lights**" (pro-revival) and "**Old Lights**" (traditional). New denominations, especially **Baptists and Methodists**, grew. New colleges — such as Princeton, Brown, Rutgers, and Dartmouth — were founded partly to train ministers.

=> Declining religious zeal; formal, intellectual preaching | Great Awakening revivals (1730s–1740s) | Splits in churches, growth of new denominations, more willingness to challenge established authority, and a shared intercolonial experience

The Awakening's biggest political effect: it taught ordinary people that they could **judge authority for themselves** and break from established churches — habits that carried into the Revolution. It was also the first event experienced across **all the colonies**.

## The Enlightenment
At the same time, educated colonists absorbed the **Enlightenment**, the European intellectual movement that stressed **reason**, science, and natural laws.

- **John Locke** (English philosopher) argued that people have **natural rights** to life, liberty, and property, and that government rests on the **consent of the governed**; if it violates those rights, people may change it. This idea runs straight into the Declaration of Independence.
- **Benjamin Franklin** embodied the colonial Enlightenment: printer, scientist (electricity experiments), inventor, and author of **Poor Richard's Almanack**.
- **Deism** — belief in a creator God who set up natural laws and then did not intervene — appealed to some elite thinkers, including Franklin and Jefferson.

!! A favorite exam contrast: the Great Awakening appealed to **emotion and faith**; the Enlightenment appealed to **reason and science**. Both encouraged people to question traditional authority.

## Imperial wars and the French and Indian War
Britain and France fought a series of wars for empire — **King William's War**, **Queen Anne's War**, and **King George's War** — with colonists and Native allies fighting in North America. The decisive one was the **French and Indian War (1754–1763)** (part of the global **Seven Years' War**).

**Cause:** both Britain and France claimed the **Ohio River Valley**. In 1754, a young Virginia militia officer, **George Washington**, clashed with French forces and was forced to surrender at **Fort Necessity**.

That same year, delegates met at Albany. **Benjamin Franklin** proposed the **Albany Plan of Union**, a council of colonies to coordinate defense (his famous "**Join, or Die**" snake cartoon promoted it). The colonial assemblies **rejected** it because they did not want to give up control over taxes — but it was an early idea of colonial unity.

The war went badly for Britain at first (General **Braddock** was killed in 1755). Then Prime Minister **William Pitt** poured money and troops into America. The British captured **Quebec (1759)**, where General **Wolfe** defeated **Montcalm** on the Plains of Abraham, and Montreal fell in 1760.

The **Treaty of Paris (1763)** ended French power in North America:
- **Britain** gained **Canada** and all French land **east of the Mississippi**, plus **Florida** from Spain.
- **Spain** received **Louisiana** (land west of the Mississippi and New Orleans) from France.

=> Rivalry over the Ohio Valley | French and Indian War (1754–1763) | Britain dominates eastern North America but has a huge war debt; Native peoples lose their French ally; Britain ends salutary neglect

## Consequences that set up the Revolution
The war left Britain with an enormous **debt** and a belief that colonists should help pay for their own defense. Colonists, meanwhile, felt proud of their contribution and no longer needed British protection from France. Many British officers had looked down on colonial soldiers, and colonists resented it. Native peoples, no longer able to play France against Britain, faced a flood of settlers. Unit 4 begins at this exact moment.
