## Why this unit matters
In 1763, colonists celebrated being British. Thirteen years later they declared independence. The exam loves this **chain of action and reaction**: a British law, a colonial protest, a British response. Learn the laws **in order**, what each did, and how colonists answered. Then learn why the outnumbered Americans won the war.

## The problem after 1763
Britain had won the French and Indian War but owed a massive **debt**, and now had to defend a huge new empire. Its leaders concluded the colonists should **pay part of the cost** and obey the trade laws they had long ignored. Salutary neglect was over.

Trouble began in the West. Native peoples of the Ohio Valley, angry at British traders and settlers, rose up in **Pontiac's Rebellion (1763)**, led by the Ottawa leader **Pontiac**, capturing several British forts. To avoid more costly wars, Britain issued the **Proclamation of 1763**, forbidding colonists to settle **west of the Appalachian Mountains**. Colonists who had fought for that land were furious, and many ignored the line.

=> Pontiac's Rebellion and the cost of frontier wars | Proclamation of 1763 bans settlement west of the Appalachians | Colonists resent limits on westward expansion

## Grenville's taxes and the Stamp Act crisis
Prime Minister **George Grenville** pushed new revenue laws:
- **Sugar Act (1764):** actually *lowered* the duty on molasses but meant to **enforce** it, with smugglers tried in **vice-admiralty courts** without juries.
- **Currency Act (1764):** banned colonial paper money as legal tender.
- **Stamp Act (1765):** the first **direct tax** on colonists — on newspapers, legal documents, licenses, playing cards. It hit lawyers, printers, and merchants, the most vocal people in the colonies.
- **Quartering Act (1765):** required colonies to house and supply British troops.

Colonists answered with the principle "**no taxation without representation**." They argued that only their own assemblies could tax them. British officials replied with **virtual representation**: Parliament represented all British subjects, even those who could not vote for it. Colonists rejected this; they believed in **actual representation** by people they elected.

Protest took many forms. Nine colonies sent delegates to the **Stamp Act Congress (1765)**, which petitioned the king. The **Sons of Liberty** intimidated stamp distributors, sometimes violently. Merchants organized **nonimportation agreements** (boycotts of British goods), and women in groups like the **Daughters of Liberty** spun homemade cloth to replace British textiles.

Boycotts hurt British merchants, who pressured Parliament. In **1766** Parliament **repealed the Stamp Act** — but the same day passed the **Declaratory Act**, asserting its power to legislate for the colonies "**in all cases whatsoever**."

## The Townshend Acts and the Boston Massacre
In **1767**, **Charles Townshend** tried "external" taxes: the **Townshend Acts** placed duties on imported **glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea**, using the money to pay royal governors and judges (freeing them from the assemblies' power of the purse). **John Dickinson's** *Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania* argued that Parliament could regulate trade but not tax for revenue. Colonists renewed boycotts.

British troops were sent to Boston. On **March 5, 1770**, a crowd taunted soldiers, who fired, killing five colonists, including **Crispus Attucks**, a man of African and Native descent. Patriots like **Samuel Adams** and **Paul Revere** (whose engraving shaped public opinion) called it the **Boston Massacre**. **John Adams** defended the soldiers in court to show that colonists respected law; most were acquitted. That same year Parliament repealed the Townshend duties **except the tax on tea**, kept as a symbol of its authority.

In 1772, Samuel Adams organized **committees of correspondence** to share news and coordinate resistance among towns — and soon among colonies. They became a network for revolution.

## Tea, punishment, and the First Continental Congress
The **Tea Act (1773)** helped the struggling **British East India Company** by letting it sell tea directly to the colonies. The tea was actually **cheaper**, even with the tax — but colonists saw a trick to make them accept Parliament's right to tax, and feared monopoly. On **December 16, 1773**, Bostonians disguised as Mohawks dumped 342 chests into the harbor: the **Boston Tea Party**.

=> Tea Act (1773) grants the East India Company a monopoly | Boston Tea Party | Parliament passes the Coercive (Intolerable) Acts to punish Massachusetts

Parliament responded with the **Coercive Acts (1774)**, which colonists called the **Intolerable Acts**: they **closed Boston Harbor** until the tea was paid for, restricted Massachusetts self-government and town meetings, allowed royal officials accused of crimes to be tried elsewhere, and expanded quartering. Separately, the **Quebec Act (1774)** extended Quebec's borders into the Ohio Valley and tolerated Catholicism there — colonists saw it as another blow.

Rather than isolating Massachusetts, the Acts united the colonies. The **First Continental Congress (1774)** met in Philadelphia (Georgia did not attend), endorsed resistance, and created the **Continental Association** to boycott British goods. It still sought rights within the empire, not independence.

## War begins
In **April 1775**, British troops marched from Boston to seize colonial weapons at Concord. Minutemen met them at **Lexington and Concord** — "the shot heard round the world." The war had begun.

The **Second Continental Congress (May 1775)** created the **Continental Army** with **George Washington** as commander, yet still sent the **Olive Branch Petition** to King George III, asking for reconciliation. The king rejected it and declared the colonies in rebellion. At **Bunker Hill (Breed's Hill)** in June 1775, colonists lost the ground but inflicted heavy British casualties.

In January **1776**, **Thomas Paine** published **Common Sense**, a best-selling pamphlet in plain language that attacked monarchy itself and argued it was "common sense" for an island to stop ruling a continent. It turned many colonists toward independence.

## The Declaration of Independence
On **July 2, 1776**, Congress voted for independence; on **July 4** it adopted the **Declaration of Independence**, written mainly by **Thomas Jefferson**. It has three parts:
1. A philosophy drawn from **John Locke**: all men are created equal with **unalienable rights** to "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness"; governments get power from the **consent of the governed**; people may alter or abolish a government that destroys these rights.
2. A long list of **grievances against King George III** (not Parliament, which colonists now denied had any authority over them).
3. A formal declaration that the colonies are "Free and Independent States."

!! The Declaration blames the **king**, not Parliament — a classic exam detail. And its Lockean phrase "consent of the governed" is a favorite passage-identification item.

## Who fought, and why the Americans won
Colonists were divided. Roughly a fifth were **Loyalists** (Tories) — often royal officials, Anglican clergy, some wealthy merchants, and recent immigrants; many fled to Canada or Britain. Most **Native nations** sided with Britain, which seemed less threatening to their lands; the **Iroquois Confederacy split**. In 1775 Virginia's royal governor issued **Lord Dunmore's Proclamation**, promising freedom to enslaved people who fought for Britain; thousands fled to British lines. About 5,000 African Americans served in the Continental forces.

The war in brief:
- **1776:** Washington lost New York but won small victories at **Trenton** and **Princeton** that kept the army alive.
- **1777:** The American victory at **Saratoga** was the **turning point** because it convinced **France** to sign an **alliance (1778)**, bringing money, troops, and a navy. Spain and the Netherlands later joined against Britain.
- **Winter 1777–1778:** the army suffered at **Valley Forge**; the Prussian officer **Baron von Steuben** drilled it into a professional force. The **Marquis de Lafayette** served as a key French volunteer.
- **1778–1781:** Britain shifted its strategy to the **South**, hoping for Loyalist support; it took Charleston, but American forces under **Nathanael Greene** wore it down.
- **1781:** At **Yorktown**, Virginia, Washington's army and French troops besieged **General Cornwallis**, while the **French navy** blocked escape by sea. Cornwallis surrendered — the last major battle.

Americans won because they fought on home ground, needed only to survive rather than conquer, had strong leadership in Washington, received crucial **French aid**, and because Britain faced a long supply line and a costly global war.

## The Treaty of Paris (1783)
The **Treaty of Paris (1783)** recognized American **independence**, set boundaries from the Atlantic to the **Mississippi River** and from Canada to Florida (which returned to Spain), granted fishing rights off Newfoundland, and recommended that states restore Loyalist property (states largely ignored this). Native allies of Britain were not even mentioned.

## A revolution in society?
The Revolution raised questions it did not fully answer. **Abigail Adams** asked her husband to "**remember the ladies**" in the new laws; women did not gain political rights but the ideal of **Republican Motherhood** (Unit 6) gave them a role raising virtuous citizens. Several northern states began to abolish slavery — **Pennsylvania** passed the first **gradual emancipation** law in **1780**. Many states ended established churches; Virginia's **Statute for Religious Freedom** (Jefferson, 1786) is the famous example.
